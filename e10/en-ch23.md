# 23: Osteoporosis: Prevalence, Risk Factors, and Management

## OSTEOPOROSIS (FIGURE 23.1A,B)

Bone is a very active organ. A continuous process, called bone remodeling, involves constant resorption (osteoclastic activity) and bone formation (osteoblastic activity) (**Figure 23.2**). Both osteoblasts and osteoclasts are derived from bone marrow progenitors: osteoblasts from mesenchymal stem cells and osteoclasts from hematopoietic white cell lineage. Cytokines are involved in this development process of bone turnover, which is regulated by the sex steroids. A system of cross-talk exists between the bone-forming osteoblasts and the bone-resorbing osteoclasts, with many autocrine, intracrine, and paracrine signals, among which receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa B ligand (RANKL), osteoprotegerin (OPG), cathepsin, and sclerostin are prominent.

The amount of bone mass at any point of time reflects the balance of the osteoblastic and osteoclastic forces, influenced by a multitude of stimulating and inhibiting agents. At puberty and during the early reproductive years, net bone gain occurs, whereas aging and a loss of

estrogen both lead to a dominance of osteoclastic activity and net bone loss (**Figure 23.3**). A decrease in calcium intake and/or absorption similarly results in bone loss, as this lowers the serum level of ionized calcium, resulting in stimulation of parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion in efforts to mobilize calcium from bone by direct stimulation of osteoclastic activity, a phenomenon known as secondary hyperparathyroidism. Increased PTH also stimulates the hepatic and renal activation of vitamin D in efforts to increase serum levels of calcium through intestinal calcium absorption. A deficiency in estrogen is associated with a greater responsiveness of bone to PTH. Thus, for any given level of PTH when estrogen is deficient, there is more calcium removed from the bone, raising serum calcium, which, in turn, lowers circulating PTH and decreases vitamin D and intestinal absorption of calcium.

**Osteoporosis,** the most prevalent bone problem in the older adults, is characterized by both decreased bone mass and decreased bone quality, and both these factors contribute to an increased risk of fragility fractures. These fragility fractures affect approximately 1.5 million individuals yearly. Osteoporosis is a major global public health problem threatening more than 44 million individuals, and it has reached epidemic levels in the United States, affecting 10 million Americans over the age of 50 (4 times more women than men).1 In addition, about 34 million people (80% women) in the United States have low bone mass (osteopenia), and they too are at an increased fracture risk.

The increase in osteoporotic fractures in the developed world is partly due to an increase in the older adult population, but not totally. A comparison of bone densities in proximal femur bones in specimens from a period of over 200 years suggests that women lose more bone today, perhaps due to less physical activity and lower parity.2 Other contributing factors include a dietary decrease in calcium and an earlier and greater loss of bone because of the impact of smoking. Our Stone Age predecessors consumed a diet high in calcium, mostly from vegetable sources.3 However, the impact of the tremendous increase in the older adult population throughout the world cannot be underestimated. Because of this

demographic change, the number of hip fractures occurring in the world each year will increase approximately 6-fold from 1990 to 2050.4

## Pathophysiology of Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is characterized by low bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration of bone tissue, leading to enhanced bone fragility and a consequent increase in the risk of fractures even with little or no trauma. The skeleton consists of two bone types. Cortical bone (the bone of the peripheral skeleton) is responsible for 80% of total bone, while trabecular bone (the bone of the axial skeleton—the spinal column, the pelvis, and the proximal femur) constitutes a honeycomb structure filled with red marrow and fat, providing greater surface area per unit volume.

**The subsequent risk of fracture from osteoporosis in women will depend on bone mass at the time of menopause and the rate of bone loss following menopause.**5 The peak bone mass is influenced by heredity and endocrine factors, among which estrogens are particularly relevant. It is now recognized that there exists only a relatively narrow window of

opportunity for maximizing bone mass. Almost all of the bone mass in the hip and the vertebral bodies is accumulated in young women by late adolescence (age 18), and the years immediately following menarche (ages 11-14) are especially important.6-8 **Estrogen exposure during adolescence is vital to bone mass accrual.** Individuals who experience a late menarche are characterized by bones with lesser density and a reduction in microstructural components, a bone quality that persists to menopause and is associated with an increased risk of fractures.9 Women who experience amenorrhea during adolescence have an increased prevalence of osteoporosis.10 Calcium supplementation in prepubertal and pubertal girls improves bone accrual, an important effect that could have long-lasting beneficial consequences.11-14

Although the onset of spinal bone loss begins in the 20s, the overall change is small until stages of menopause transition and early menopause.15,16 Bone density in the femur peaks in the mid- to late 20s and begins to decrease around age 30. In general, trabecular bone resorption and formation occur 4 to 8 times as fast as in cortical bone. Beyond age 30, trabecular resorption begins to exceed formation by about 0.7% per year. Bone loss accelerates after menopause as up to 5% of trabecular bone and 1% to 1.5% of total bone mass loss occur per year in the first years after menopause. This accelerated loss continues for about 5 years, after which bone loss rate is considerably diminished but continues as aging-related loss.17 For the first 20 years following cessation of menses, post-menopause-related bone loss results in as much as 50% reduction in trabecular bone and a 30% reduction in cortical bone.18,19

**When estrogen levels decline, bone remodeling, resorption, and formation increase.** The site of maximal bone remodeling is determined by a need to adapt to physical loads on the skeleton. Each remodeling locus is initiated by osteoclast-mediated reabsorption and excavation of old bone, a process that takes 3 to 5 months, followed by osteoblast-mediated restoration and refilling. In reproductive years, the processes of bone resorption and bone formation are well balanced with resultant maintenance of net bone mass. Estrogen exerts a tonic suppression of remodeling by directing activated osteoclasts toward apoptosis and maintains a balance between osteoclastic and osteoblastic activity; in the absence of estrogen, osteoclastic activity predominates, resulting in net bone resorption. The

precise mechanism of action for sex steroid protection of bones remains unknown; however, a growing body of knowledge indicates complex interactions at the molecular level, with both a classic pathway involving genomic transcription mediated via hormone receptor signaling, and a nongenomic pathway that inhibits apoptosis (**Figure 23.4**).20,21 Increased efficiency of calcium absorption, probably secondary to estrogen-induced enhancement of the availability of vitamin D, and a direct role for the estrogen receptors in the osteoblasts are likely important factors. Many estrogen-dependent growth factors and cytokines are involved in bone remodeling.22,23 Estrogen modulates the production of bone-resorbing cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1 and IL-6, bone-stimulating factors such as insulin-like growth factor 1, colony-stimulating factor, OPG, and proteins that are members of the transforming growth factor-β family.24 Estrogen increases vitamin D receptors in osteoblasts, and this may be a means by which estrogen modulates vitamin D activity in the bone.25 There is little evidence that estrogen affects bone by altering the circulating calciotropic hormones.26 Thus, the actions of estrogen are primarily direct effects on bone and important effects on vitamin D metabolism and renal and intestinal handling of calcium.

Estrogen is a critical hormone for skeletal health in both males and females. Individuals of male genotype (XY) who have mutations in the estrogen receptor-a or who have aromatase deficiencies are known to grow slowly and have markedly reduced bone densities.27,28 Analysis of the decline in testosterone and circulating estrogen levels with aging indicates that the amount of bioavailable estrogen circulating in the blood is the most consistent predictor of bone density in men and women.29 And most impressively, men with an aromatase deficiency, treated with estrogen, demonstrated that both androgens and estrogens are necessary in order for biological males to reach optimal bone mass.30,31

Racial differences in bone mass are well recognized. Bone mass, adjusted for body size, is greater in the Black compared to White populations. It is believed that racial differences in bone density are established in childhood and early adolescence.32,33 More than half the difference, comparing Black children with White children, is due to differences in body size and composition; a significant contribution is made by bone metabolism, a smaller portion by sex hormone levels, and only 2%

of the difference is unexplained.34 During adolescence, Black people absorb calcium more efficiently and bone turnover is greater with more net bone formation.35

In general, bone mass is greater in Black women who have obesity and less in White slender-framed women with sedentary lifestyle. Spinal bone density is similar among Black and Asian women (lower in White women), and hip bone density is higher among Black women compared to White and Asian women.36 Conventional wisdom has held that Asian women have even lower bone densities than White women. The Study of Women’s Health Across the Nation (SWAN) study demonstrated that the apparent racial differences disappeared when the data were adjusted for body size.36,37 Both Black and Asian women have higher bone density and lower fracture rates than White women. Despite the greater bone mass and lower fracture rates, the impact of osteoporotic fractures in Black and Asian women is still considerable. Postmenopausal estrogen therapy is associated with improved bone mineral density (BMD) and protection against fracture risk regardless

of race.38 Japanese women demonstrate the same amount and pattern of bone loss after menopause as White women.39

Although estrogen plays a principal role in regulating bone density, genetic susceptibility for low bone mass and that for bone loss are important. A study of the premenopausal daughters of women with osteoporosis revealed lesser bone mass in daughters of osteoporotic women, suggesting genetic influence as well as the sharing of a lifestyle that may contribute to a relatively low peak bone mass.40 **Studies of twins and mother-daughter pairs indicate that up to 70% of the variation in bone density is determined by heredity.**41

Variations in the gene that encodes the vitamin D receptor are prevalent in postmenopausal women with decreased bone densities.42 The absence of vitamin D receptor gene allelic polymorphisms was reported in older women who neither lose significant bone nor respond to calcium supplementation.43 The inherited aspects of osteoporosis are likely to be influenced by multiple susceptibility genes. For example, low bone density is associated with specific alleles of *COLIA1*, one of the two genes that encode the two polypeptides of collagen.44 Two single nucleotide polymorphisms, variants for key biologic proteins (the OPG and lipoprotein receptor-related genes), are present in slightly over 20% of White people and are associated with an increased risk for osteoporosis.45 Clarity on genetic markers to identify individuals at increased risk for osteoporosis is still lacking and informative yield of existing efforts is, at best, limited.

The accelerated loss of bone in postmenopausal women is largely attributed to estrogen loss; 75% or more of the bone loss that occurs in women during the first 15 years after menopause is due to estrogen deficiency rather than to aging itself.46,47 Vertebral bone is especially vulnerable, beginning to decline as early as 20 years of age.48 Vertebral bone mass is significantly decreased in perimenopausal and early postmenopausal women who have rising follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and decreasing estrogen levels, whereas bone loss from the radius is not observed until at least a year after menopause.49 This early loss of axial skeleton bone mass suggests that the hypoestrogenic postmenopausal state is not the only cause of vertebral osteoporosis.50 One obvious suspect is a decline in dietary intake of calcium and vitamin D in the premenopausal years; nevertheless, menopause and the loss of estrogen remain the major

contributors to bone loss. The risk of fracture, therefore, depends on two factors: the bone mass achieved at maturity and the subsequent rate of bone loss. **A high rate of bone loss after menopause (the “fast loser”) is highly predictive of an increased risk of fracture.** The combination of a low bone mass and fast losing is additive, and thus, these individuals are at the highest risk of fracture. Fast losing probably reflects lower endogenous estrogen levels. Bone density, which is the threshold for vertebral fractures, is only slightly below the lower limit of normal for premenopausal women.51 **Figure 23.5** outlines the various factors contributing to osteoporosis.

## Bone Loss During the Menopausal Transition

Should a clinician be concerned about bone loss and consider interventions during the perimenopausal years? A decline in BMD as well as upregulation of circulating and urinary levels of CTX (C-telopeptide), a marker representing degradation of bone collagen, is apparent in the menopause transition, well before the final menstrual period (FMP). Some studies concluded that calcium supplementation of perimenopausal women retards metacarpal and lumbar bone loss.52,53 Perimenopausal women in the SWAN study demonstrated decreasing bone densities that correlated with increasing FSH levels; however, accelerated bone loss did not occur until late in the perimenopause.37 The amount of perimenopausal bone loss is small unless estrogen levels are below normal.54-57 Unlike those in perimenopause (discussed earlier in this chapter), otherwise healthy women who are anovulatory or those who demonstrate inadequate luteal phase ovarian function (and thus are exposed to less progesterone) but are not hypergonadotropic do not have an increase in bone loss.58,59 **Bone loss in the perimenopausal period is unlikely to be clinically consequential for the majority and interventions and treatments to prevent future osteoporosis are not necessary in perimenopausal women who have adequate estrogen levels and who are not deemed at an exaggerated risk for low bone mass (such as those with a known eating disorder).** However, those who had failed to attain optimal peak bone mass in their younger years, an earlier onset of bone loss in the menopause transition, may contribute toward an enhanced lifetime fracture risk; such women will benefit from an individualized risk assessment and consideration of timely initiation of antifracture interventions (discussed later in this chapter).

## Signs and Symptoms of Osteoporosis

*Fragility fracture* is a fracture that results from minimal trauma, or from a person falling from a standing height. Fragility fractures are the most common consequence of osteoporosis and commonly involved anatomic sites such as the humerus, upper femur, distal forearm, and ribs. Osteoporosis is recognized as a “silent disease” that, in the absence of targeted screening, may only present itself once the affected individual sustains a fracture. Osteoporosis is responsible for more than 2 million fractures per year in the United States.1 About one in two White women

over age 50 will experience an osteoporosis-related fracture. Lack of estrogen and advancing age are both causative to the cumulative fracture burden of postmenopausal years. Back pain is a major clinical symptom of vertebral compression fractures. The pain with a fracture is acute, and then it decreases over 2 to 3 months, but lingers as chronic low back pain due to increasing lumbar lordosis. The pain subsides within 6 months unless multiple fractures produce a picture of constant pain. **Many individuals will not know they have osteoporosis until they experience a fracture.** Loss of height, kyphosis, decreased mobility, and even respiratory compromise and worsening constipation are recognized disabilities resulting from fractures of the thoracolumbar spine.

**Epidemiologic studies have revealed the following**60,61**:**

1. **Spinal (vertebral) compression fracture.** Symptomatic spinal osteoporosis, causing pain, loss of height, postural deformities (the kyphotic dowager hump) with consequent pulmonary,

gastrointestinal, and bladder dysfunction, is 5 times more common in White women than men. Approximately 50% of women over 65 years of age have spinal compression fractures; about two-thirds are clinically unrecognized. **Each complete compression fracture of a vertebral body** (**Figure 23.6**) **causes the loss of approximately 1 cm in height.** The average untreated postmenopausal White woman can expect to shrink 2.5 in (6.4 cm) over the course of post-menopausal years. The most common sites for vertebral fractures are the 12th thoracic and the first three lumbar vertebrae. These physical changes also have a negative impact on body image and self-esteem.

1. **Colles fracture.** There is a 10-fold increase in distal forearm fracture (**Figure 23.7**) in women as they progress from age 35 to 60 years. White women are more predisposed to wrist fracture than those of other races; a White woman has approximately a 15% lifetime risk of a forearm fracture. Colles fractures are the most common fractures among women until age 75, when hip fractures become more common.

Postmenopausal women who have already experienced a vertebral fracture deserve aggressive intervention. The risk of subsequent, additional vertebral fractures is substantial; 20% of women experience another vertebral fracture within 1 year after the first fracture.68 These women are also at increased risk for nonvertebral fractures.69,70 **A complete evaluation for osteoporosis is an essential component of care for a patient presenting with a fragility fracture**. As defined by the World Health Organization, a fragility **fracture** is one that results from an injury that would be insufficient to **break** a normal bone. **All osteoporotic fractures are associated with an increased risk of mortality that persists for 5 to 10 years after the fracture.**71

Because there are so many women with osteoporosis, a greater prevalence of depression in this population would amount to a clinical problem of considerable proportions. A cross-sectional subset in a large clinical trial reported a greater prevalence of depressive symptoms in women with fractures.72 It is well recognized that fractures secondary to osteoporosis are accompanied by a reduction in psychological and physical well-being. As far as depression goes, it is difficult to know which came first, depression or fractures, leading to subsequent depression. It has been reported that depressed people have a greater incidence of falls,73 and thus it is not unreasonable to consider that depression comes first in some people. Furthermore, depressed people are often sedentary and eat poorly, factors that favor bone loss. It is speculated that chronically increased cortisol levels associated with depression might contribute to bone loss, similar to that observed with the pharmacologic administration of glucocorticoids. On the other hand, a cohort study of American women, despite finding a link between depression and fractures, failed to detect an increase in depression associated with lower bone density measurements.73 However, other studies *have* reported increases in depression associated with lower bone densities.74-76

Bone loss has been documented in an established rodent model for stress-induced depression, characterized by a decrease in osteoblastic bone formation that can be attenuated by an antidepressant drug.77 In this experimental model, osteoblastic inhibition was mediated by stress-induced stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system. Although this response is

associated with an increased secretion of adrenal glucocorticoids, the evidence also indicates a direct role for sympathetic fibers in bone.

**We need to be aware that women who have experienced fractures may have depressive symptoms, and appropriate interventions can have a beneficial impact on quality of life. The important point is that depression and fractures are linked and attention to psychological well-being should be an integral part of management of individuals who have experienced fragility fractures as each condition can impact the course of the other in different patients, and both directly impact on the quality of life in all affected individuals.**

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are the favored treatment for depression in older adults, a problem that affects about 10% of the older population. Several earlier studies had reported an increased risk of fractures with the daily use of SSRIs; however, these earlier studies were unable to control for the various factors that influence this risk, especially falls, depression, and bone density. An excellent prospective cohort study in Canada indicated that the increase in fractures persisted after controlling for these factors.78

There is a direct effect of SSRIs on bone. Components of the neural system are involved in bone metabolism, and serotonin receptors and serotonin transport have been identified in osteoblasts and osteocytes. The bone effects of PTH and mechanical stimulation are modulated by the serotonin system. Mice with a mutation for the serotonin transporter develop less bone mass and strength.79 Therefore, daily SSRI use can impair bone formation, tilting the balance in favor of resorption and bone loss; indeed, decreased bone densities have been reported in both male and female SSRI users (but not in users of tricyclic antidepressants).80,81

## Measuring Bone Mineral Density

There is a 50% to 100% increase in fracture risk for each standard deviation decline in bone mass (approximately 0.1 g/cm bone mass).82 Evidence of lower bone mass at the hip is even more predictive of fracture risk; lesser bone mass by one standard deviation at the hip has been associated with nearly a 3-fold increase in risk of fracture.83 **Although low bone density reliably predicts the risk of fracture, increases in bone density in**

**response to treatment do not demonstrate a direct correlation with a reduction in fractures. Therefore, a few percentage point differences achieved by various treatments may have little clinical meaning.**

The impressive correlation between fracture risk and low bone density has raised the question of whether it is of value to screen for osteoporosis. Keep in mind that because the rate of bone loss after menopause contributes as much to the risk of fracture as the total bone mass present at the time of menopause, a normal bone density measurement at the time of menopause does not mean that the patient will not be at risk of fracture later in life. A relatively young woman with a low bone mass could be targeted for appropriate intervention; however, it is not cost-effective to attempt to screen all postmenopausal women with an expensive such as dual X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) that is considered the gold standard for bone density assessment. Cost effective and simpler technologies such as single-photon, single-energy x-ray absorptiometry and ultrasonography can offer reliable fracture risk assessment that in low resource settings can be considered for assessing fracture risk.84

Bone density measurements are certainly useful when the information will facilitate an individual to make an informed decision regarding initiating specific treatment, such as postmenopausal hormone therapy. Indeed, a decision to use hormone therapy and better maintenance of a hormone program are correlated with patients’ knowledge of their bone density measurements.85,86 In the United States, multiple organizations including the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG),87 the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), and the National Osteoporosis Foundation (NOF) are unified in their recommendations that in the absence of risk factors, postmenopausal women should be offered screening for osteoporosis by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) starting at age 65; earlier screening should be considered in those with risk factors, especially if results of the bone density test can help determine the course of management. Patients who have received long-term corticosteroid, thyroxine, anticonvulsant, or heparin treatment warrant bone mass assessment earlier than age 65. **After an initial assessment of bone density, the decision to perform repeat measurements should be individualized based on results of the initial test and tailored *to the patient’s clinical picture*;** for the majority, repeat

bone density measurements to monitor bone loss can be considered every 2-to 5-year intervals.

Key Points: Reasons to Measure Bone Mass

Standard x-rays do not provide an early assessment of fracture risk; 30% to 40% of bone must be lost before radiographic changes become apparent. Photon absorptiometry measures the transmission of photons through bone. Single-photon absorptiometry uses a (131)I source of energy or, more recently, miniature x-ray tubes. These methods can reliably measure bone density in the radius and the calcaneus and are relatively inexpensive. These measurements correlate with vertebral bone density and predict the risk of future fracture, particularly in older women.84 DXA employs different photons from two energy sources. DXA provides good precision for all sites of osteoporotic fractures, and the radiation dose is much less than for a standard chest x-ray. Whole-body scans by DXA can measure total body calcium, lean body mass, and fat mass. **DXA (Figure 23.10) remains the gold standard technology for bone density measurement in routine clinical practice.** Quantitative computed tomography (CT) for bone density measurements can be performed on most commercial CT systems; however, radiation exposure is higher than with DXA, and measurements of the femur are not available, although very accurate measurements of the spine are possible.

**FIGURE 23.10**

For high precision, the best information is provided by the DXA technique (together with sophisticated software to give a precision of 1%), measuring the three sites of greatest interest, the radius, the hip, and the spine.88 Better accuracy is gained by three-site assessments because there can be differences among the anatomic sites. In other words, a normal value at one site does not preclude a low bone density at another site. For practical clinical use (and for screening), measurements are made at the lumbar spine, the hip, and the femoral neck. Serial measurements are best made at least 2 years apart to allow meaningful interpretation of interval data. Measurement of the bone density at the radius or calcaneus is more cost-effective, using single-source x-ray absorptiometry, and can be used for screening. It is anticipated that ultrasonography will prove to be a low-cost, effective method for bone mass assessment, particularly in high-risk populations.89 Ultrasonographic measurements of the calcaneus have been

reported to be as accurate as femoral neck measurements by DXA in predicting the risk of hip fractures.90,91 However, peripheral measurements are not accurate for monitoring response to treatment.

## Diagnostic Tests

Patients with osteoporosis should be screened for underlying conditions that can lead to bone loss:

1. Careful history and, when indicated, appropriate laboratory studies to rule out long-term use of bone-losing medications, alcohol abuse, metastatic cancer, and chronic liver disease
2. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D: for vitamin D deficiency
3. Serum PTH, calcium, phosphorus, and alkaline phosphatase: for primary hyperparathyroidism
4. Renal function tests: for secondary hyperparathyroidism with chronic renal failure
5. Thyroid function tests: for hyperthyroidism and excessive thyroid hormone treatment
6. Blood count and smear, sedimentation rate, and protein electrophoresis: for multiple myeloma, leukemia, or lymphoma

Excess thyroxine treatment can cause bone loss. However, retrospective studies of thyroid function and bone mass have not produced uniform conclusions.93 In the Study of Osteoporotic Fractures research study, women with a previous history of hyperthyroidism had an increased risk of subsequent hip fractures, and women taking thyroid hormone also had an increased risk of fracture (which did not reach statistical significance).94 However, in a prospective assessment of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels in the Study of Osteoporotic Fractures, no association could be detected between low TSH and bone loss.95 A recent meta-analysis provides evidence of increased fracture risk for the spine, hip, and wrist in both subclinical hyperthyroidism and subclinical hypothyroidism.96 **It is thus prudent to screen for thyroid dysfunction in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis as well as those with osteopenia and maintain TSH levels within the normal range**.

***Should Osteopenia Be Treated?***

The term “low bone mass” is now preferred to the older term “osteopenia,” as defined by bone density *T*-scores between −1.0 and −2.5. Approximately 40% of American postmenopausal women have bone densities in the range

of osteopenia. It is logical to expect a continuum of fracture risk extending from normal bone density to osteoporosis, and a longitudinal American study observed that osteopenia was associated with a 1.8-fold higher rate of fracture (compared with a 4-fold higher rate with osteoporosis).97 Experts argue, however, that most women with osteopenia simply have lower than average bone mass but will not have progressive bone loss and, in the absence of risk factors, may not require any bone-specific interventions beyond lifestyle modifications that include attention to diet, calcium and vitamin D intake, and weight-bearing exercise. Notably, an ancillary study of the VITamin D and omegA-3 triaL (VITAL), which examined vitamin D and omega 3 supplementation in women older than 55 years who did not have low BMD, found that supplementation led to a lower risk of fractures compared to placebo.98 Consideration for treatment depends on the demonstration of continued bone loss as determined by declining bone density on serial measurements. **We support preventive drug therapy for postmenopausal women with evidence of low bone mass in the presence of one or more risk factors for fracture or documented progressive bone loss.**

***FRAX, the World Health Organization Fracture Risk Assessment Tool***

The World Health Organization fracture risk assessment tool, FRAX is updated and calibrated according to risk in different geographic areas. This tool is available online for free use from the Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation (<https://www.bonesource.org/frax-tool>) or directly from the FRAX website (<http://www.shef.ac.uk/FRAX/>).99,100 The FRAX algorithm predicts the likelihood for an older individual to experience a fragility fracture over a period of 10 years because of low bone mass. There is consensus in the field that treatment is cost-effective if FRAX predicts a 10-year hip fracture probability of at least 3% or an overall risk of any osteoporotic fracture greater than 20%.101 The clinical application of FRAX produces no major changes in already-existing guidelines.102 An osteoporotic bone density and historical risk factors continue to be important factors indicating the need for treatment. **FRAX is useful in identifying which individuals with osteopenia merit treatment**; nevertheless, a careful evaluation of risk factors as reviewed in this chapter continues to be a reliable method to indicate fracture risk in an individual

with low bone mass. For example, long-term use of corticosteroid or treatment with aromatase inhibitors (eg, in the management of postmenopausal breast cancer) identifies individuals in whom bone loss prevention should be considered regardless of the bone density. In addition, fracture risk calculation by FRAX utilizes bone density at the hip, but not at the spine. The decision to initiate antifracture treatment as well as choice of treatment in a patient with low bone density at the spine but with normal hip bone density requires careful consideration of the individual’s history and risk factors. FRAX does not replace good medical judgment. **FRAX can provide a reliable risk assessment even in the absence of BMD data as it takes into consideration clinically relevant history in risk score calculation (Table 23.2). We support preventive drug therapy for postmenopausal women with evidence of low bone mass and a 10-year hip fracture probability of 3% or higher or a 10-year fracture probability of 20% or higher for any osteoporosis-related fracture according to FRAX.**

***Biochemical Markers of Bone Turnover***

There are many serum and urinary biochemical markers of bone turnover (see **Table 23.3**). Some of the biomarkers (such as hydroxyproline, pyridinoline, and deoxypyridinoline cross-links of collagen, telopeptides of collagen, and serum cross-linked telopeptides) reflect the activity of the osteoclasts, whereas others (such as osteocalcin, bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, and procollagen peptide) reflect the activity of the osteoblasts. The urinary tests use a second-morning voided urine specimen, and the immunoassays measure hydroxyproline, pyridinoline, deoxypyridinoline, the collagen cross-linked N-telopeptides, or the collagen cross-linked CTXs.103 These are structural proteins released by osteoclastic resorption of bone. **A measurement of bone turnover markers provides a snapshot in time of the current state of bone metabolism.** Because of individual variability, marker measurements correlate poorly with BMD; however, changes in these markers quickly indicate responses to therapy.104 Therefore, it has been proposed that efficacy of therapy (with either hormones or other antiresorptive agents) can be judged by comparing a baseline value with a single follow-up measurement after 1 to 3 months of treatment.105 A lesser decrease in the serum or urinary cross-linked peptides

indicates a less than optimal bone response, raising concerns about compliance with therapy as well as identifying patients for further evaluation and specific added treatment. How reliable, practical, and cost-effective this approach is for routine clinical use is a reasonable question. Upregulation of bone resorption markers identifies a state of excessive bone loss, which if left unchecked will result in progressive decline in bone mass. Notably, fracture risk is higher in individuals with evidence of low bone density and upregulated bone loss compared to either alone. **Single, random measurements of bone markers are not useful in predicting future bone loss, and bone turnover markers are insensitive indicators of response to raloxifene and calcitonin. Measurement of bone turnover markers is best confined to clinical trials, except in special situations as noted in this chapter.**

## TREATMENT OPTIONS FOR FRACTURE RISK REDUCTION

### Menopausal Hormone Treatment

Hormone therapy is an effective bone-protecting strategy that improves BMD and offers fracture risk reduction. Antifracture efficacy of hormone therapy is now documented by randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trials.106-108 The Women’s Health Initiative (WHI; discussed in detail in chapter 22) reported fracture outcomes based on an average of 5.6 years of follow-up, comparing placebo treatment with daily 0.625 mg conjugated estrogen and 2.5 mg medroxyprogesterone acetate (similar results were also reported in the estrogen-only arm of the clinical trial)108,109 (**Table 23.4**).

Kaplan-Meier estimates of the impact of hormone therapy in WHI indicated that the reduction in fractures continued to increase over time, suggesting that a very powerful effect would be achieved with treatment of a long duration. In addition, spinal fractures included only clinically symptomatic fractures (known to represent about one-third of vertebral fractures); again, the overall effect was probably greater if all vertebral fractures had been included. The beneficial effect was achieved even though the use of a bisphosphonate increased from 1% at baseline to about 6% in the estrogen-progestin group and 10% in the placebo group. The effect would have been even greater if bisphosphonates had not been prescribed by the participants’ clinicians. In the WHI, hormone therapy

decreased the risk of hip fracture by 60% among women with adequate calcium intake at baseline, but not in those with a lower intake. This underscores the importance of emphasizing to patients the consequences of inadequate calcium and vitamin D intake. The impact of hormone therapy was greater in leaner women, as one would expect. **Thus, the results of the WHI provide us with clinical trial evidence for a therapy that protects against fractures in a population not selected for having osteoporosis.** Hormone therapy use in the United States declined by at least 50% following the initial publications from the WHI, and this decrease was paralleled by a significant increase in the incidence of fractures among postmenopausal women!110

Estrogen therapy stabilizes the bone turnover in favor of net bone gain through suppression of bone resorption. Besides inhibiting osteoclastic resorption activity, estrogen increases intestinal calcium absorption, increases 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (the active form of vitamin D), increases renal conservation of calcium, and supports the survival of osteoblasts. With estrogen therapy, one can expect a 50% to 60% decrease in fractures of the arm and hip,111-114 and when estrogen is supplemented with calcium, an 80% reduction in vertebral compression fractures can be observed.115 This reduction is seen primarily in patients who have taken estrogen for more than 5 years.116,117 **Protection against fractures wanes**

**with advancing age, and long-term estrogen use is necessary to maximally reduce the risk of fracture after age 75.**

Because most osteoporotic fractures occur late in life, women and clinicians must understand that the short-term use of estrogen immediately after menopause cannot be expected to offer sustained protection against fractures in the seventh and eighth decades of life. Some long-term protection is achieved with 7 to 10 years of estrogen therapy after menopause, but the impact is minimal after age 75.118 In a prospective cohort study of women 65 years of age and older, in those who had stopped using estrogen, and in those who were over 75 and had stopped estrogen after more than 10 years of use, there was no substantial effect on the risk for fractures.119 Any substantial fracture risk reduction effect from hormone therapy requires treatment initiation within 5 years of onset of menopause and for current use to extend into the older adult years. **The protective effect of estrogen rapidly dissipates after treatment is stopped because estrogen withdrawal is followed by rapid bone loss.** In the 3- to 5-year period following loss of estrogen, whether after menopause or after cessation of estrogen therapy, there is an accelerated loss of bone.120-124 The Postmenopausal Estrogen/Progestin Interventions (PEPI) trial, however, found that this rate of bone loss was comparable to that in women not on hormone therapy,125 but in a Swedish case-control study, most of the beneficial effect of hormone therapy was lost 5 years after discontinuing treatment.111 An American randomized trial concluded that older adult women who stopped taking hormones lost most of the bone density gained while on hormone therapy.126 Two reports documented that less than 4 years of exposure to estrogen is followed by a period of time, perhaps 10 years, when bone density is better and fractures are less prevalent than nonusers of hormone therapy.127,128 This protection is not as great as that obtained with long-term hormone use, and beneficial effects should not be expected after age 70, when fractures are most prevalent.

Maximal protection against osteoporotic fractures, therefore, requires lifelong therapy; even some long-term protection requires 10 or more years of treatment, and some protection against fractures is lost within 5 years of discontinuation.129,130 Standard doses of systemic estrogens that are commonly utilized for symptom control offer skeletal benefit, regardless of route of administration.111 A study of women randomized to treatment either

with continuous transdermal delivery of estradiol 50 mg daily or oral estrogen demonstrated that both equally prevented postmenopausal bone loss.131

In the PEPI trial, at the end of 3 years, the women receiving hormone treatment had experienced about a 5% gain in BMD in the spine and 2% in the hip compared with approximately a 2% loss in the placebo group.132 How long does this gain in bone density continue in hormone-using women? In a 10-year follow-up study of women receiving estrogen-progestin therapy, the spinal bone density steadily increased, reaching a level 13% over baseline after 10 years of treatment.133 In another cohort study, hormone users did experience a loss of bone at the hip over a 10-year period of time; however, the incidence of hip, wrist, and vertebral fractures was significantly reduced in the hormone users.134

The idea of postponing treatment to prevent osteoporosis until later in life has some merit. Changes in bone density in the early postmenopausal years have no major effect on fractures later in life, except in individuals who already have low bone density. This amounts to only 5% of women in their early postmenopausal years, and most of this 5% will have risk factors such as smoking, history of fracture at a younger age, lean body mass, or excessive alcohol consumption. The positive impact of hormone therapy on bone has been demonstrated in women over age 65 and even over age 75.116-119,135-137 This may be an argument in favor of treating very old women with estrogen for skeletal benefit, as estrogen use between the ages of 65 and 74 has been documented to protect against fractures.112 However, starting hormone treatment later in life and solely for the benefit of bones is a controversial issue, especially in light of the WHI hormone trials. A large cohort study could detect no significant impact on nonspinal fractures.119 In a later analysis of this same cohort, some reduction in fracture risk was observed in women starting hormone therapy after age 60, but a statistically significant reduction was present only in those who started treatment before age 60 and remained on it.138

Older studies suggested that a dose of 0.625 mg of conjugated estrogens is necessary to preserve bone density and that an estradiol blood level of 40 to 60 pg/mL is required to protect against bone loss.139-141 We now know that any amount of estrogen can have an impact, although it is likely that some degree of protection is lost when doses are less than the equivalent of

0.625 mg conjugated estrogens or 1.0 mg estradiol.142,143 Estradiol levels as low as 10 pg/mL have a beneficial impact on bone density compared with values below 5 pg/mL. **Thus, any increment in estrogen, even within the usual postmenopausal range, will exert protective effects.** This explains how a positive effect on bone was observed even with the utilization of the vaginal ring that delivers a very small amount of estradiol with minimal systemic absorption.144

A lower dose of 0.3 mg daily of conjugated estrogens or 0.5 mg estradiol prevented loss of vertebral trabecular bone when combined with calcium supplementation (to achieve a total intake of 1,500 mg daily).145-147 In a small study without calcium supplementation, daily administration of

0.3 mg conjugated estrogens and 2.5 mg medroxyprogesterone acetate produced a slight increase in lumbar bone density with a lesser effect on the hip.148 Even a dose of estradiol as low as 0.25 mg/d produced an increase in bone density.149

The Women’s Health, Osteoporosis, Progestin, Estrogen (HOPE) trial involved 800 postmenopausal women in a dose-response evaluation of conjugated estrogens and medroxyprogesterone acetate. The lowest dose,

0.3 mg conjugated estrogens, either unopposed or combined with 1.5 mg medroxyprogesterone acetate, produced a gain in bone density.150 A major concern with lower doses is the possibility that there will be a significant percentage of nonresponders (discussed later). Nevertheless, a lower dose of estrogen may be more acceptable (fewer side effects) in older adult women.

Certain progestational agents have been reported to act independently, in a manner similar to estrogen, to reduce bone resorption.151 When added to estrogen, progestins can lead to an apparent synergistic increase in bone formation associated with a positive balance of calcium.152-155 On the other hand, other studies have failed to find a greater impact on bone, comparing estrogen alone to estrogen plus a progestin.119 These different results are explained by the fact that the synergistic result of combining estrogen with a progestin is determined by the type of progestin, being limited to members of the 19-nortestosterone (norethindrone) family.156 This could reflect an increase in free estrogen levels because of a reduction in sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG). Careful studies indicate that the

addition of medroxyprogesterone provides an extra effect on bone density only in women with established, significant osteoporosis.155,157,158

In the PEPI trial, at the end of 3 years, there was little difference comparing treatment with estrogen only to the groups receiving either sequential or continuous combinations of estrogen and medroxyprogesterone acetate or micronized progesterone.132 Thus, the daily, continuous combination of estrogen-progestin is equally efficacious in maintaining bone density as the sequential regimens, although at least one study has indicated a slightly greater response in the lumbar spine with continuous treatment.156,159-161

The addition of testosterone to an estrogen therapy program has been reported to provide no additional beneficial impact on bone or on relief from hot flushes.162,163 Others have demonstrated a greater increase in bone density with an estrogen-androgen combination compared with estrogen alone, although the blood estrogen levels achieved were higher than those associated with standard postmenopausal hormone therapy.164 In another study, only a very high pharmacologic dose of methyltestosterone enhanced the bone density achieved with estrogen alone.165

**We support the consideration of early initiation of menopausal hormone therapy in early menopausal women with evidence of low bone mass as a fracture risk reduction strategy, but caution that consideration for prolonged use of hormone regimens in postmenopausal women must be individualized. If long-term use of menopausal hormone therapy is chosen as the preferred approach to fracture risk reduction, prescribers are cautioned on the importance of periodic reassessments and timely adoption of risk reduction strategies (such as switching from oral to transdermal route and reduction in dose of estrogen) to ensure that patients electing to be treated with hormones achieve protection against osteoporotic fractures while mitigating estrogen-related risks. Testosterone should not be prescribed with the expectation that androgen treatment will improve bone health.**

### Follow-Up Assessment of Hormone-Treated Women

Either a stable bone density or an increase indicates successful treatment; however, not all women will maintain or gain bone density on postmenopausal hormone therapy; in one study, 12% of treated women lost

bone despite apparently good compliance.166 In the PEPI 3-year clinical trial, where compliance rates were probably maximal, 4% of treated women lost bone in the spine and 6% in the hip.132 **It is worthwhile to repeat bone density assessment following initiation of hormone therapy for skeletal benefit to assess response in treated women when they are in their late 60s**. This problem of poor response is discussed near the end of this section.

### Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulators

***Raloxifene***

Raloxifene is the prototype selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) that is commonly utilized in the management of osteoporosis. SERMs act as agonists-antagonists at the level of estrogen receptors and can have selective actions on specific target tissues. Raloxifene produces favorable estrogen-like responses in bone and on lipids, but unlike estrogen, it is neutral at the level of the endometrium.167,168 In contrast to estrogen, raloxifene also has proven efficacy in reducing risk for breast cancer. The changes in bone remodeling produced by raloxifene are consistent with an estrogen agonist effect.169 As with estrogen therapy, discontinuation of raloxifene treatment is followed by a resumption of bone loss.170

The increase in bone density associated with raloxifene, 60 mg daily, is less than that seen with bisphosphonate, alendronate.171-173 The Multiple Outcomes of Raloxifene Evaluation (MORE) study of raloxifene administration to women who are osteoporotic reported results from 8 years of follow-up.174,175 Women with low bone density *T*-scores had approximately a 50% reduction in vertebral fractures with raloxifene treatment; those with previous vertebral fractures had approximately a 35% reduction. However, **there has been no evidence of a reduction in hip or wrist fractures with raloxifene use**. Although the reduction in vertebral fractures is similar to that seen with alendronate and estrogen, why is there no decrease in hip fractures, despite a bone density response that is only slightly less than that associated with alendronate? There are at least two possible explanations. The study was of insufficient duration to demonstrate an impact in a population that was relatively young for hip fractures. Or a weaker impact on bone density produces a lesser effect in the hip, which

has a combination of cortical and trabecular bone that is less responsive compared with the spine with its large content of trabecular bone.

A reduction in vertebral fractures with raloxifene that is equivalent to that with estrogen or bisphosphonates despite a lesser increase in bone density is strong evidence that fracture risk and reduction are not simply reflections just of bone density. Thus, a few percentage point differences comparing bone densities between two therapies do not translate into a difference in fracture protection. For this reason, fracture data are important, and we cannot conclude that small differences predict lesser or better performances in fracture protection. Another example is the greater gain in bone density associated with combination treatment with alendronate and estrogen; a greater gain in bone density does not necessarily mean a greater protection against fractures. Similar to estrogen, use of raloxifene has been associated with a small but real risk for thrombotic events. **In our view, raloxifene is an option for prevention of osteoporosis-related spinal fractures, especially for those who are deemed at an enhanced risk for both breast cancer and spine fracture risk. We recommend, however, periodic evaluation of bone density in the hip, as if ongoing site-specific bone loss continues despite compliance with raloxifene, patient and clinician should consider another treatment option that offers broader skeletal benefits at the level of the hip as well as the spine.**

***Bazedoxifene***

Bazedoxifene acetate (BZA) is a third-generation SERM, with tissue-specific estrogen agonist-antagonist effects. It has favorable effects on bone and lipids, but does not affect the endometrium or the breast. BZA in a dose of 20 mg daily decreased the risk of all clinical fractures in a randomized clinical trial, with a potency comparable to other antiresorptive agents in postmenopausal women at high risk for fractures.176,177 In a subgroup of women at higher risk for fractures, BZA had a reduced risk of nonvertebral fractures (50% reduction with 20 mg), compared with *both* raloxifene and placebo. The only adverse event that differed with treatment was an increase in venous thrombosis with treatment compared with placebo. The results of this trial indicate that the effect of BZA on bone should be comparable to that of estrogen and bisphosphonates.

The reduction of nonvertebral fractures with BZA compared with raloxifene should not be ignored. We have known for some time that even with 8 years of follow-up, raloxifene has no impact on the risk of hip fractures. This is likely because raloxifene is less potent, and thus the hip with a mixture of cortical and trabecular bone is more resistant to raloxifene’s effects, compared with the spinal column that is composed of sensitive, trabecular bone. As discussed in chapter 22, BZA partnered with conjugated equine estrogen is the first tissue-selective estrogen complex (TSEC) that is available in the United States for menopause management. The idea is to gain the benefits of estrogen (BZA has little impact on hot flushes), protect the endometrium and possibly the breast, and enhance some actions of estrogen, such as a reduction in fractures. This approach to postmenopausal hormone therapy has eliminated a concomitant need for progestational agents in hormone-using postmenopausal women with an intact uterus. **In our opinion, the combination of BZA and estrogen is a promising option for reducing the risk of osteoporosis; its efficacy in reducing fracture risk, however, remains unclear, and future studies are awaited to determine how risk reduction benefit of TSEC against fractures, if any, compares to that of traditional hormone therapy.**

**Calcium Supplementation**

There has been considerable confusion over whether calcium supplementation by itself can offer protection against postmenopausal osteoporosis. This is partly due to the fact that calcium studies were performed in women who were in the very early postmenopausal years, in the midst of the rapid loss of calcium associated with estrogen deficiency, and this estrogen effect overwhelmed any responses to calcium. Studies that involved women beyond this early stage of the postmenopausal period indicated a positive impact of calcium supplementation.178-181

Calcium absorption decreases with age because of a decrease in biologically active vitamin D and becomes significantly impaired after menopause. A positive calcium balance is mandatory to achieve adequate prevention against osteoporosis. Calcium supplementation (1,000 mg/d) reduces bone loss and decreases fractures, especially in individuals with low daily intakes.182,183 Estrogen acts to improve calcium absorption by

increasing the levels of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D and makes it possible to utilize effective supplemental calcium in lower doses (**Figure 23.11**).

In order to remain in zero calcium balance, postmenopausal women require a total of 1,200 mg/d elemental calcium (through diet and supplements).182,184,185

Because the average woman without any dietary restrictions receives about 500 to 700 mg of calcium in her diet, the minimal daily supplement for women on estrogen equals an additional 500 mg.

Key Points: Calcium Supplementation

High-dose calcium supplementation can unmask asymptomatic hyperparathyroidism, causing abnormally high calcium blood levels and an increased risk for renal stones.186,187 Assessment of dietary intake and restrictions should be undertaken at routine clinical visits, and recommendations on dose of supplementation should be individualized based on dietary intake. Women receiving calcium supplementation in excess of 500 mg daily should have their blood levels of calcium and phosphorus measured yearly for the first 2 years. If normal, no further surveillance is necessary.

Even with the commonly used therapeutic doses of calcium, nearly 40% of postmenopausal women have inefficient absorption.188 Estrogen improves calcium absorption and allows the utilization of supplemental calcium in effective doses without the side effects associated with higher doses (constipation and flatulence) that diminish compliance. **We must emphasize that although calcium supplementation is important, it cannot provide the same degree of protection against osteoporosis as that achieved by hormonal therapy or raloxifene or agents discussed later in this section.**189,190 Nevertheless, the beneficial impact of estrogen on bone is reduced in the absence of calcium supplementation.191 Skeletal harm

from low calcium intake in the aging population gets compounded if there is coexisting insufficient dietary intake of proteins.192

Improved calcium intake in adolescents results in significant increases in bone density and skeletal mass, providing protection against osteoporosis later in life.193,194 Calcium supplementation is far more important during adolescence than in the reproductive years when bone formation is minimal. Under age 25, during the years of bone accumulation, the recommended daily calcium intake is 1,300 mg.195 This amount, 1,300 mg/d, is also recommended during pregnancy and lactation. Most calcium comes from dairy products; relying on other foods is not easy because it requires a high intake volume of other foods to provide the same amount of calcium in

There are dozens of calcium supplements on the market, containing calcium carbonate, calcium lactate, calcium phosphate, or calcium gluconate. Calcium carbonate tablets are the cheapest and contain the most elemental calcium (40%). Calcium lactate tablets contain 13% calcium, calcium citrate 23%, and calcium gluconate only 9%. The calcium carbonate antacids are excellent, inexpensive sources. Bone meal and dolomite as sources of calcium should be avoided because they are contaminated with lead. Calcium citrate does not require gastric acid for absorption and is the best choice for older patients with reduced gastric acid production. Calcium supplementation is most efficient when single doses do not exceed 500 mg and when taken with a meal. **Excess calcium supplementation (especially not with meals) is associated with a slight increase in risk for kidney stones**.186,197

**Vitamin D**

Changes in vitamin D and calcium metabolism accompany aging and contribute to the aging-related increase in skeletal fragility.198,199 There is an age-related decrease in the ability of tissues to convert the major circulating form of vitamin D, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, to the active form of vitamin D (1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol, better known as 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D), and there is a decrease in the ability of the intestine to absorb dietary vitamin D. Exposure of the skin to ultraviolet rays in sunlight stimulates the formation of cholecalciferol (vitamin D3). One dose of sunlight that

produces erythema is equivalent to 10,000 to 25,000 IU of vitamin D. Excessive exposure to sunlight cannot cause toxic levels of vitamin D because any excess in vitamin D3 is inactivated by ultraviolet radiation.

Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) in commercial supplements has been replaced with the more effective vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol), which is more than 3 times the potency of vitamin D2.200 Cholecalciferol and ergocalciferol are converted in the liver to calcitriol (25-hydroxyvitamin D), which is the major circulating metabolite of vitamin D, and its levels in circulation are reflective of the overall vitamin D status. The circulating level of 25-hydroxyvitamin D and renal conversion to its active form, 1,25-

dihydroxyvitamin D, are regulated by calcium, phosphorous, and PTH and 1,25 dihydroxyvitamin D plays a major role in stimulation the absorption of calcium and phosphate from the gastrointestinal tract (**Figure 23.12**).

**The importance of optimization of vitamin D status through lifestyle modifications and judicious use of supplementation in aging populations cannot be minimized. For postmenopausal women, the recommended daily allowance for vitamin D is 600 IU.**195 **However, it is important to appreciate that recommended dietary allowance (RDA) doses are inadequate to achieve correction of underlying vitamin D deficiency, and there is sufficient evidence to support safety of daily intake of 1,000 to 2,000 units of vitamin D in addition to calcium supplementation.** Older studies that failed to document a beneficial impact of vitamin D supplementation were handicapped by doses that were too low and in the setting of inadequate calcium intake.201 A pooled analysis of 68,500 patients in seven major vitamin D fracture trials concluded that vitamin D in appropriate doses coupled with calcium supplementation reduced fractures at all sites in men and women.202

**FIGURE 23.12**

Because adequate and active vitamin D depends on cutaneous generation mediated by sun exposure, women in the winter months can easily be relatively vitamin D deficient and lose bone.203 In far northern and southern areas, the winter sunlight is inadequate to stimulate dermal activation. But even in areas of the world where there is adequate sun exposure, inadequate dietary intake and an indoor lifestyle yield a significant number of women with abnormally low levels of circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D.204 The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) documented a rising prevalence of vitamin D insufficiency in the United States over the last two decades.205 **If uncertain regarding vitamin D supplementation, the serum level of 25-hydroxyvitamin D can be measured; although the Institute of Medicine (IOM) in the United States has lowered the threshold to define vitamin D deficiency to less than 20 ng/mL, the U.S. Endocrine Society maintains that a level below 30 ng/mL is abnormal.**113,206

The benefit of vitamin D supplementation is clear in older postmenopausal women, and the lack of side effects encourages us to recommend vitamin D supplementation as part of the overall program for osteoporosis prevention in younger postmenopausal women. But keep in mind that effective bone response to vitamin D requires adequate calcium intake.

One caution regarding vitamins: excessive intake of vitamin A, specifically retinol, was reported to be associated with an increased rate of hip fractures in the women not using hormone therapy in the Nurses’ Health Study; it would be better to avoid obtaining vitamin D by taking more than one multivitamin tablet daily.207 It has been argued that excess retinol intake may explain the higher rate of fractures in Scandinavia.208

The WHI conducted a randomized trial of calcium and vitamin D supplementation.209 The 36,282 postmenopausal women were part of the WHI clinical trials involving postmenopausal hormone therapy or dietary modification. The average follow-up was 7 years. Thirty-seven percent of the women were ages 50 to 59, 45.5% were 60 to 59, and 17.5% were 70 to

79. The treated group was supplemented with 1,000 mg calcium and 400 IU vitamin D daily. Overall analysis indicated no significant reduction in fractures with calcium/vitamin D treatment. The women treated with

calcium/vitamin D had a 17% greater risk of kidney stones. However, a closer look reveals some good news:

* Correcting for compliance by analyzing just those women who continued their medication revealed a statistically significant 29% reduction in risk for hip fractures. Only 59% of the treated women at the end of the trial were taking the intended dose.
* Women who were 60 years and older had a 21% significant reduction in hip fractures.
* The reduction in hip fractures was greatest (42%) in those women who combined calcium/vitamin D supplementation with postmenopausal hormone therapy.

The population at greatest risk for fractures (the oldest women in the WHI study) actually benefited, and the study confirmed something already known: that hormone therapy combined with calcium/vitamin D supplementation achieves the best results. Keep in mind that the women in this study were not at high risk for fractures. Indeed, whole-body and spinal bone density increased in the placebo group. This is hard to explain; the average postmenopausal untreated woman loses spinal bone density. In this study, only hip bone density demonstrated a loss, and thus, it is not surprising that significant benefits were demonstrated only with hip fractures. The fact that most of these women were overweight probably contributed to the protection against bone loss in the spine. In a population of women losing bone density in both the hip and spine, and in women with other risk factors for fractures, calcium/vitamin D supplementation should yield even better results than those reported by the WHI, including a reduction in spinal and arm fractures. Degenerative joint disease and the resulting osteophytes can convey false reassurance of improved bone density on radiologic imaging including DXA, and this may have contributed to the improved spine bone density observed in the WHI placebo group.

What about the kidney stones? The women in the WHI trial were allowed to continue their own programs of supplementation. Thus, many took calcium and multivitamins (which contain 400 IU vitamin D). The average daily calcium intake of the study population was 1,100 to 1,200

mg, 2-fold higher than the average American woman. The WHI does not provide data to answer this most important question: Was the small increase in kidney stones observed in women taking excessive amounts of calcium and vitamin D?

The WHI has published multiple findings from the calcium/vitamin D randomized trial. The conclusions are uniformly negative, summarized as follows:

* The incidence of invasive breast cancer was similar in the treated and placebo groups. The risk of breast cancer was not associated with baseline 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels.210
* Calcium and vitamin D supplementation did not protect against a decline in physical functioning or performance.211
* The incidence of newly diagnosed diabetes was the same in the treated and placebo groups.212
* Myocardial infarction and stroke events were similar in the treated and placebo groups.213
* Calcium and vitamin D supplementation had no effect on blood pressure or the risk of developing hypertension.214

The WHI also reported the effects on overall mortality in their randomized trial of calcium and vitamin D supplementation.215 After an average follow-up of 7 years, there was no impact on total mortality (744 deaths in treated women and 807 deaths in the placebo group). There was a nonsignificant reduction (about 10%) in risks for stroke and cancer mortality. Comparing the women older than age 70 to those younger than age 70, there was a lower risk for mortality in the younger group, but this did not achieve statistical significance. When analysis was restricted to those participants who were adherent to therapy, the results for overall mortality and for the younger women were essentially unchanged. However, a nested case-control study assessed the results according to serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels at baseline; the results indicated that women with the lowest levels had a higher risk for death. The WHI investigators concluded that their results weakly support the hypothesis that calcium and vitamin D supplementation modestly reduces cancer and

cardiovascular mortality. Overall, the results indicated no major benefit of calcium and vitamin D supplementation on the risk of death due to cancer or cardiovascular disease.

Smaller trials and observational studies of vitamin D supplementation have demonstrated reductions in total mortality, reductions in blood pressure and cholesterol levels, and reduced risks of stroke and coronary heart disease (CHD). Vitamin D insufficiency has been linked to a wide spectrum of problems, including an increase in autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, upper respiratory tract infections, diabetes mellitus, multiple sclerosis, cancer, and cardiovascular disease. Low levels of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D have been reported to be associated with higher all-cause mortality, greater prevalence of breast cancer, peripheral arterial disease, and higher cardiovascular mortality.216-220 It is proposed that many tissues produce locally the active 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D that exerts beneficial effects on cell regulation and differentiation before being inactivated within cells without reaching the circulation and interfering with calcium metabolism.199

What could explain the lack of strong agreement between the WHI results and the rest of the literature? The answer can be found in the characteristics of the women in the WHI and the doses of calcium and vitamin D used for supplementation. The women in the WHI were not at high risk for fractures, and most of the women were overweight. Only one-third of the participants had a low calcium intake at entry to the study, and 29% were already taking calcium supplementation. As previously noted, the average daily calcium intake of the study population was 1,100 to 1,200 mg, 2-fold higher than the average American woman.209 Low serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D were found in only 25% of the women in whom it was measured.

The negative publicity associated with the WHI publications has obscured the importance of fracture risk in women. In the observational arm of the WHI, the risk of fracture was compared to the risks of cardiovascular events and breast cancer in a prospective cohort of 83,724 women, aged 70 to 79.221 **The recorded events indicated that the number of women who experienced a fracture in 1 year’s time exceeded the combined number of women experiencing invasive breast cancer or cardiovascular disease (except in Black women in whom cardiovascular events dominated).**

And don’t forget that in the WHI, the women adherent to their calcium/vitamin D supplementation had a 29% reduction in risk for hip fractures, and the reduction in hip fractures (42%) was greatest in those women who combined calcium/vitamin D supplementation with hormone therapy.209

Because 60% of individuals who live in northern latitudes have low serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the WHI results do not rule out the possibility of beneficial effects of adequate supplementation in a large segment of our population.222 The WHI nested case-control study documented that the risk of hip fracture steadily increases in women as their serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D decrease.223

**Vitamin D is essential for adequate calcium absorption and maximal bone protection, but remember that supplemental vitamin D requires adequate calcium intake.**201,202 Calcium absorption decreases and PTH increases as the levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D fall below 30 ng/mL. Multiple studies have demonstrated that vitamin D together with calcium supplementation in older men and women reduces the rate of fractures.189,224,225 Normal vitamin D levels are important for muscle functioning; vitamin D supplementation alone and in combination with calcium lowers the risk of falling and improves lower extremity function in older men and women.226,227 **We believe that vitamin D and calcium supplementation are simple yet critical strategies that offer risk reduction against fragility fractures through mechanisms that include positive effects on BMD, on bone quality, and possibly on muscular function. Supplementation strategies must utilize dietary sources as well as supplements, and dosing regimens should be individualized to the needs of the population at risk.**

Clinicians should be vigilant toward identifying populations at risk for vitamin D deficiency and be more aggressive in confirming their impression through monitoring serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D. While a level less than 20 ng/mL is a definitive indication of vitamin D deficiency, any value less than 30 ng/mL is below normal and

Key Points: Vitamin D

must be interpreted in the context of the overall picture. We believe that this measurement should be part of every older individual’s annual medical assessment.

The choice of vitamin D supplementation dose and regimen should be dictated by the severity of deficiency, and periodic surveillance is advised to ensure effectiveness of chosen regimen and to safeguard against a small but real risk of overdosing. It takes about 3 months after initiation of supplementation for serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D to reach a new steady state after a change in dose.

Patients who lose bone despite adequate treatment for bone loss must have serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D and calcium measurements because inadequate calcium and vitamin D can be an easily correctable reason for suboptimal response to effective regimens.

Given these facts, how can we determine how much vitamin D to give to an individual patient? A collection of bone experts reached a consensus by considering the amount of vitamin D supplementation required to change blood levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D and PTH, correlating this information with the levels of vitamin D required to prevent bone loss.228 The U.S. IOM recommends intake of 600 IU as the daily allowance of vitamin D for postmenopausal women until age 70, with an increase in recommended allowance to 800 IU daily for women 71 and older. However, in women with documented evidence of vitamin D deficiency (serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D <30 ng/mL), doses as high as 1,500 to 2,000 IU/d can be safely utilized to achieve levels required to maintain the optimal serum level of 25-hydroxyvitamin D greater than 30 ng/mL. Short durations (3 months) of higher dose regimens are often utilized to restore low levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D to normal, such as 50,000 IU vitamin D2 (available as

prescribed formulation in the United States) administered once or twice weekly for 3 to 6 months followed by a switch to lower dose daily regimens of 600 to 2,000 IU. Toxic doses of vitamin D (enough to produce hypercalcemia) are far beyond these recommendations.

**Bisphosphonates**

Bisphosphonates are effective in preventing bone loss by enhancing osteoclast apoptosis and inhibiting bone resorption. The bisphosphonates share a basic phosphate-carbon-phosphate core and bind avidly to the bone

mineral calcium where they remain for many years, making bone less susceptible to osteoclastic action. The first generation of bisphosphonates (etidronate) also inhibited bone mineralization, and therefore intermittent therapy was necessary. The newer generations of bisphosphonates allow bone formation to occur while inhibiting bone resorption and make it possible to use continuous therapy rather than intermittent therapy.

**Diagnostic screening with the laboratory tests previously noted is important before the administration of bone-specific drugs in order to avoid inappropriate treatment in the presence of a secondary cause for osteoporosis, especially renal disease.**

Oral bisphosphonates must be taken on an empty stomach with a full glass only of water, no other liquid, at least 30 minutes before any other food or liquid intake in order to achieve adequate absorption. A failure to remain upright for at least 30 to 60 minutes and until after the first food intake of the day after ingesting bisphosphonates can result in esophageal injuries, such as esophagitis, esophageal ulcers, and esophageal erosions with bleeding.229 Upper gastrointestinal problems in clinical trials (with carefully instructed and monitored subjects) are similar when comparing alendronate treatment with placebo, indicating that improper usage is the culprit.230,231 Risedronate (Actonel), 5 mg daily, is as effective as alendronate (Fosamax) for the prevention of bone loss, provides similar protection against fractures, and may be better tolerated.232-236 Other equally effective bisphosphonates include ibandronate (Boniva) and zoledronic acid (Reclast, Aclasta). Notably, zoledronic acid administered as a single 5-mg dose intravenously protects against bone loss for at least 2 years.237,238

The mechanism for the gastrointestinal reactions is an interference with the normal healing process that repairs trauma associated with eating. With less frequent exposure, this healing is allowed to proceed unimpeded. Periodic administration with equivalent bone efficacy is possible because of the high affinity of bisphosphonates for bone. Thus, weekly administration of alendronate and risedronate, each in a dose of 35 mg for prevention and 70 mg for treatment, reduces side effects and produces similar increases in bone density compared with a daily regimen.239-243

In women with osteoporosis, alendronate (10 mg daily) administration reduced the risk of all subsequent fractures by 30% and vertebral fractures by 50% in 3 to 4 years of treatment.244-246 In normal postmenopausal

women, alendronate increased bone density in both the spine and the hip, and the 5-mg dose (the preferred dose for preventive treatment) was more effective than the 2.5 mg dose.247,248 In the data derived from follow-up of 4,432 women for an average of 4.2 years, a statistically significant reduced risk of fracture was demonstrated only in women with initial *T*-scores of

−2.5 or less, a 36% reduction in all fractures and a 50% reduction in vertebral fractures.249

Bisphosphonate treatment obviously benefits women who already have a low bone density or previous vertebral fractures. Risedronate reduces vertebral and hip fractures in women with osteoporosis with an impact similar to that of alendronate.234,250 After 5 years of treatment with risedronate, the risk of new vertebral fracture decreased by 59%, an improvement from the 49% reduction reported after 3 years.251 Ibandronate reduced vertebral fractures by about 60% over 3 years; zoledronic acid reduced vertebral fractures by 70% and hip fractures by 41% over 3 years.252,253 However, a retrospective cohort analysis of over 196,000 women did note a higher risk of atypical femur fracture in patients taking bisphosphonates, especially over several years.

The Early Postmenopausal Interventional Cohort (EPIC) study concluded that over a 4-year period of time, alendronate and hormone therapy in the United States produce similar bone density results. The greater increase noted in Europe with hormone therapy probably reflects the use of 19-nortestosterone progestins, which are known to have an additive effect on bone density when combined with estrogen. Combining bisphosphonate and hormone therapy produces an added gain in bone density. When women who were already taking hormone therapy also received alendronate (10 mg) for 1 year, the gain in bone density ranged from 0.9% in the femoral neck to 2.6% in the spine.254 In women with osteopenia, combined therapy with alendronate 10 mg and 0.625 mg conjugated estrogens produced a 1% to 2% greater gain in bone density over a 2-year period of treatment; similar results were reported in a 1-year trial with risedronate.255,256 **There are no data available on whether addition of a bisphosphonate to estrogen regimen improves the antifracture efficacy of either agent alone. Furthermore, there is a concern that oversuppression of resorption can ultimately yield more brittle bones.**

Compliance with alendronate has been overestimated by the clinical trials. It is well recognized that participants in clinical trials are better motivated, are better supported, and perform better. In the Kaiser Permanente Medical Care Program in California, about one-third of patients had acid-related complaints, and one in eight required treatment.257 About 50% of the patients in Kaiser did not comply with instructions, and about 50% discontinued therapy by 1 year.257,258 Analysis of general pharmaceutical claims databases in the United States revealed that only 43% refilled their first prescription, and after 2 years, only 20% were adherent to treatment.259 Bone density measurements are recommended to assess compliance and to provide motivation for continuation; compliance can also be assessed by measuring bone markers prior to initiation of therapy and then repeating assessment 1 to 3 months later; a 30% decrease in bone markers is anticipated if patients are complaint with prescribed strategy and there are no major issues of malabsorption. Programs with good patient support have reported long-term compliance with hormone therapy: 65% at 7.5 years in an Australian population and 61% at 7 years in the United Kingdom.260,261

Because of the apparent benefits associated with alendronate therapy, the clinical trials were halted after 4 years, although follow-up has indicated persistent gains in BMD through 10 years of use.262 There is evidence that a bisphosphonate already in the bone can recirculate when bone containing the bisphosphonate is remodeled.263 Thus, perhaps long-term treatment with this class of agents is unnecessary. Comparing bone loss after discontinuation of treatment, accelerated bone loss occurs after estrogen and raloxifene, but a residual effect on bone density is maintained for up to 7 years after alendronate is discontinued170,264-266 (**Table 23.6**).

***How Long Should Bisphosphonate Treatment Be Continued?***

In an extension of the alendronate Fracture Intervention Trial (FIT), the participants were randomized after 5 years of treatment either to another 5 years of treatment or placebo.267 The group that discontinued alendronate treatment (5 or 10 mg/d) experienced small losses of BMD over 5 years. The levels, however, remained above the pretreatment levels 10 years previously. There were no differences in nonvertebral fractures between the treatment and placebo groups, but there was a 2-fold higher rate of clinically recognizable vertebral fractures in the placebo group (5.3% vs 2.4%). It was concluded that for most women, a period of drug holiday may be safely considered after 5 years of treatment with alendronate and that long-term treatment beyond 5 years should be limited to high-risk women (women with existing vertebral fractures or very low bone densities).

The rate of hip fracture among women who discontinued bisphosphonate therapy was compared with women who remained on treatment.268 The study group consisted of 9,063 users who had been compliant for at least 2 years. The hip fracture rate almost doubled in the women who discontinued treatment compared with those who did not. After discontinuation for a year or longer, the risk of hip fracture increased by 9 months in women with low compliance rates. In women with high

compliance rates for 2 or 3 years, there were no significant differences in fracture risk after discontinuation for up to 1 year later. Why is there this lingering effect?

The unique tight binding of bisphosphonates to bone matrix causes this class of drugs to remain in the body for decades. This is believed to be the explanation for why there is no rapid bone loss after discontinuing bisphosphonate treatment in contrast to the rapid loss that follows the termination of treatments such as estrogen or raloxifene or denosumab (discussed later). This is also the reason why concerns have been raised regarding long-term treatment because when bone remodeling releases bound bisphosphonate, it is free to be active again, and as a result, the endogenous bisphosphonate is added to the administered bisphosphonate, raising dosage exposure. The potential risk that has been long recognized is that prolonged exposure to bisphosphonates or excessive dosage would oversuppress bone resorption, thus oversuppressing bone turnover, a phenomenon referred to as *adynamic* bone that ultimately affects the biomechanical strength of bone and allows microcracks to accumulate. A unique type of fracture of the femur, commonly referred to as “atypical fracture,” is characterized by a transverse fracture in an area marked by hypertrophy of the cortex and has been associated with long-term use of bisphosphonates.269

***Bisphosphonates and Osteonecrosis of the Jaw***

Osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ) is a rare complication that has been described with long-term use of potent antiresorptive agents, including bisphosphonates and denosumab.270 In an analysis of information from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) databank linked to Medicare claims, 16,703 patients with cancer were identified who were treated with intravenous (IV) bisphosphonates (pamidronate and zoledronic acid) from 1995 to 2003.271 When 14,349 treated patients were matched with 28,698 controls, the treated group had a 3-fold increased risk of jaw or facial bone surgery and a very large increased risk of osteomyelitis of the jaw. The estimated absolute risk equaled 5.48 events per 100 patients over 6 years. In addition, the risk increased with increasing cumulative dose. The absolute risk is much less in patients without cancer being treated with bisphosphonates for the management of osteoporosis, in whom the

incidence of ONJ is estimated at 0.001% to 0.01%, minimally higher than in the general population.270

The mechanism underlying ONJ in bisphosphonate users is uncertain beyond the recognition that infection and blood flow changes are involved. It is postulated that a compromised healing ability of the bone because of inhibition of bone turnover leads to sequestered osteomyelitis and necrosis. Studies have suggested that the risk of ONJ is greater in patients treated with zoledronic acid compared with pamidronate.272-274 Awareness of this problem has led to increased attention to oral hygiene and the avoidance of tooth extractions in the high-risk population of patients with cancer receiving bisphosphonate treatment.

Treatment related risks of osteomyelitis and ONJ have long been recognized, although controversy remains regarding a causative role of bisphosphonates for these risks. To be sure, most of the cases have been in patients with cancer, usually treated with IV high doses, but this complication was also reported in patients receiving oral bisphosphonate treatment for osteoporosis, with no history or evidence of malignancy.274,275 Experts in the field point out that many clinicians specializing in osteoporosis have never seen a case. **The incidence of ONJ with oral bisphosphonates is somewhere from 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 100,000.** They further argue that a true cause-and-effect relationship would require appropriate controlled studies, and two are ongoing. But clinical decision-making cannot be delayed until data are available, and it is by no means certain that the ongoing trials will yield definitive results on such a rare event. Patients should be cautioned regarding this rare problem and urged to practice good dental care. **We recommend consideration for dental evaluation and cleaning for patients in whom initiation of bisphosphonates for management of osteoporosis is being considered, prior to initiation of therapy, attention to oral hygiene during the course of treatment, and ongoing periodical surveillance.**276

***Bisphosphonates and Atrial Fibrillation***

An increase in the incidence of new-onset atrial fibrillation has been linked with the use of bisphosphonates, more so with IV than oral regimens.277 A meta-analysis presented at the meeting of the American College of Chest Physicians in October 2008 estimated that atrial fibrillation is observed in

2.5% to 3% of individuals treated with bisphosphonates and that 1% to 2% experience hospitalization or death. A case-control study concluded atrial fibrillation could be attributed to bisphosphonates in 3% of previous or current users.278 These positive reports were followed by a series of consistently negative studies. A larger Danish case-control study concluded that there was no link between atrial fibrillation and the use of bisphosphonates.279 A Danish cohort study of patients with fracture found no increase in the risks of stroke or myocardial infarction and concluded that an increase in atrial fibrillation could be attributed to the use of bisphosphonates in individuals already at increased risk for cardiovascular events.280 In two large American cohort databases of patients undergoing coronary angiography, there was no increase in atrial fibrillation or myocardial infarction associated with bisphosphonate treatment.281 A retrospective cohort study from Taiwan compared bisphosphonate use with the use of raloxifene and reported a similar rate of atrial fibrillation in both treatment groups.282 An apparent increase in atrial fibrillation can be attributed to the fact that most bisphosphonate users are older, have more cardiovascular disease, and are at greater risk for atrial fibrillation. Nonetheless, caution is advised in the older adult and those at risk, such as those with known hypertensive disorders, ischemic heart disease, cardiomyopathy, and valvular heart disease.

***Bisphosphonates and Esophageal Cancer***

In a Letter to the Editor in the January 1, 2009, issue of the *New England Journal of Medicine*, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) reported 23 cases (8 fatal) of esophageal cancer in patients being treated with alendronate.283 As of 2009, a total of 31 cases of esophageal cancer had been collected in Europe and Japan associated with alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and etidronate. Although this is a small number of cases with drugs that have been used for more than a decade by millions of people, the concern has added credibility because of the well-recognized side effect of esophageal injury with oral bisphosphonates. Nevertheless, the FDA report was anecdotal in nature, with no comparison group. In response, American and European comparisons of treated patients to the

U.S. expected incidence of esophageal cancer or to nontreated patients in national databases in the United Kingdom and Denmark failed to detect an

increase in esophageal cancer in bisphosphonate-treated patients.284,285 A meta-analysis of 11 studies published in 2015 was similarly reassuring and failed to identify any evidence to support an increase in risk of upper gastrointestinal cancers with the use of bisphosphonates.286

***Bisphosphonates and Pain***

The FDA has informed clinicians that patients taking bisphosphonates rarely experience severe bone, joint, or muscle pain that is relieved when treatment stops; discontinuation of bisphosphonate treatment and consideration for an alternative agent are advised in this setting.

Bisphosphonate treatment is best reserved for older postmenopausal women. It is not a drug class of choice for the prevention of osteoporosis in relatively young postmenopausal women in whom hormone therapy may be preferentially considered as a first-line approach for osteoporosis prevention and fracture risk reduction.

In all except very-high-risk patients being treated with bisphosphonates, it would be wise to consider a time limit for the duration of exposure. Bone density should be measured after 2 to 4 years of treatment, and a period of “drug holiday” should be considered after 5 years of use for those who are not deemed at a markedly high fracture risk. Patients should be followed by monitoring bone density, with a resumption of treatment in those who rapidly lose bone or in those who accumulate a loss of 5% to 10% in 1 year.

The onset of severe pain at any site is an indication to discontinue bisphosphonate treatment.

In the absence of demonstrated fracture reduction benefit, the increased cost and likely increase in adverse events from a combination regimen (such as estrogen and bisphosphonate), such an approach is not currently recommended.

**Denosumab**

Denosumab (Prolia) is a human monoclonal antibody to RANKL and is one of the newest of the available antifracture agents that was approved for the

treatment of osteoporosis in high-risk patients. RANKL is secreted by osteoblasts and binds to its receptor, RANK, on the surface of osteoclasts, stimulating the osteoclasts to mature and to resorb bone (**Figure 23.2**). OPG is a soluble receptor that is produced by the osteoblasts and that acts as a sponge to bind circulating RANKL, thereby preventing the RANK-mediated activation of osteoclasts; in the absence of RANK signaling, osteoclasts undergo apoptosis. An agent that could act like OPG, therefore, would prevent bone loss. Denosumab works like OPG, but rather than competing with RANK for its activator RANKL, it binds with great affinity to RANKL, thus preventing activation of RANK. The antiresorptive effects of denosumab are thus distinct from those of bisphosphonates and estrogens. Denosumab prevents both maturation and survival of osteoclasts and active bone resorption by osteoclasts.

The impact of denosumab on bone density has been evaluated in phase 2 clinical trials.287 The participants were postmenopausal women up to age 80 who had osteoporosis based on low *T*-scores. For the first 2 years, the patients were randomized to one of seven denosumab doses, alendronate, or placebo. After 2 years, patients in five of the treated groups were continued for 2 more years on 60 mg daily given subcutaneously only once every 6 months. One treatment arm was discontinued, and then after a year, treatment was resumed with 60 mg denosumab every 6 months. One treatment arm was discontinued after 2 years and followed. Two hundred and sixty-two women completed the study. Long-term treatment increased bone density in the spine and hip, whereas the placebo group continued to lose bone density. A reduction in markers for bone turnover was sustained. The group that discontinued treatment after 2 years lost bone density; after a year, retreatment with denosumab restored the bone to the level of the gain achieved in the first 2 years, levels similar to the groups who continued treatment for 4 years. No significant differences in the treatment and placebo groups were noted for adverse events. Similar results have been reported in a phase 3 trial with 332 women in 21 centers in Canada and the United States.288 In a large, 3-year, worldwide multicenter trial involving 7,868 women with osteoporosis, the Fracture Reduction Evaluation of Denosumab in Osteoporosis Every 6 Months (FREEDOM) trial, treatment with 60 mg denosumab subcutaneously every 6 months reduced vertebral fractures by 68%, hip fractures by 40%, and other nonvertebral fractures by

20%.289 A separate phase 3 trial concluded that denosumab treatment increased BMD at the hip and spine significantly more than alendronate.290 There were no differences in major adverse events in these trials, including osteonecrosis, or indications of suppressed immune function; however, eczema and cellulitis were significantly increased in the worldwide study.289 The data representing 10 years of denosumab exposure (3 years of exposure in the FREEDOM trial plus 7-year exposure in the open-label extension study) offer reassurance regarding safety and efficacy of long-term use. Not only did the incidence of fractures in the long-term users remain comparable to that observed during the FREEDOM trial; additionally, there was evidence of ongoing improvement in BMD during the 10-year period of exposure. Adverse event profile was similarly comparable to that encountered in the 3-year FREEDOM trial.291

How do the results compare with bisphosphonates? The increase in bone density at the spine and hip is greater with denosumab, and total body bone density, as exemplified by the radius, increases with denosumab, but not with bisphosphonates. **After discontinuation of denosumab, however, bone loss begins immediately and the concept of drug holiday does not hold for denosumab. Prescribing information warns of the possibility of an increased risk of multiple vertebral fractures following discontinuation.**292 This quick reversibility contrasts with bisphosphonates that bind tightly to bone, remaining in bone for decades and maintaining a long-term effect. The 6-month dosing regimen of denosumab and subcutaneous route of administration offer convenience. Alternatively, the requirement for subcutaneous administration might seem to be an obstacle to some, but on the other hand, a required visit to the office might increase continuation rates. **Given that data on safety and ongoing efficacy of denosumab beyond 10 years of use are wanting, in our opinion, it is prudent to then consider switching to a bisphosphonate to ensure that the skeletal benefit gained from denosumab use persists beyond its discontinuation.**

**Calcitonin**

Calcitonin regulates plasma calcium by inhibiting bone resorption and has been long recognized to offer fracture risk reduction. The antifracture

efficacy of calcitonin is inferior to the agents discussed earlier. In a randomized 5-year trial, calcitonin treatment reduced spinal fractures with less of an effect (about 33%) compared with estrogen, alendronate, and raloxifene and had no reduction in hip fractures.293 Studies with intranasal delivery of salmon calcitonin (200 IU daily) indicate it can increase bone density. Human calcitonin is available, but recombinant salmon calcitonin (Fortical and Miacalcin) is more potent. Calcitonin treatment should be combined with vitamin D and calcium supplementation. The lack of a dose-response and a high dropout rate raised questions regarding the potency and efficacy of calcitonin in preventing fractures. Calcitonin, however, still has a place for use in patients who are unable to tolerate alternative agents, as well as for patients with intractable back pain, given its analgesic potential.294

**Fluoride**

The addition of fluoride, a potent stimulator of bone formation, can offer significant protection against osteoporosis. The clinical response depends on the formulation and dose. Slow-release sodium fluoride (25 mg bid given 12 of every 14 months) combined with calcium supplementation reduced the vertebral fracture rate with essentially no side effects.295 Treatment is recommended for no longer than 4 years to avoid the toxic accumulation of fluoride in bone.296 This treatment is reserved for patients with established postmenopausal osteoporosis; however, a commercial preparation has not yet been approved.

**Strontium**

A divalent strontium salt of ranelic acid acts through uncoupling and rebalancing of the bone turnover in favor of net bone formation. A dose of 2 g of strontium ranelate is dissolved in water and taken at bedtime. Clinical trials indicate that a reduction in spinal and nonvertebral fractures is achieved comparable to antiresorptive agents.297,298 Antifracture efficacy has been reported with up to 8 years of use. The main side effect is nausea and diarrhea that usually resolves in a few months. In pooled analyses, an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) was associated with strontium ranelate relative to placebo over 5 years of treatment, and

ongoing caution is advised. Strontium is not approved for the management of osteoporosis in the United States, but oral strontium ranelate is available in many countries (Protelos, Protos, Protaxos, Bivalos, Osseor) for use in the management of osteoporosis.299

**Tibolone**

Tibolone is structurally related to the 19-nortestosterone progestins used clinically in oral contraceptives. Its chemistry, mechanisms of action, and clinical effects were discussed in detail earlier in this chapter. Tibolone prevents bone loss in postmenopausal women as effectively as estrogen or estrogen-progestin therapy.300-306 In a large U.S. dose-response study with doses ranging from 0.3 to 2.5 mg daily, only the 1.25- and 2.5-mg doses produced progressive bone density increases in the femoral neck. Indeed, the impact on bone was essentially the same for the two highest doses, 1.25 and 2.5 mg. Although the 1.25-mg dose is acceptable for the prevention of bone loss, the 2.5-mg dose is more effective for the alleviation of hot flushes.307

The beneficial impact on bone can be attributed to tibolone’s estrogenic metabolites acting through the estrogen receptor because it is blocked by an antiestrogen, but not by an antiandrogen or an antiprogestin.308 Tibolone prevents the bone loss associated with gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist treatment (and the side effect of hot flushing).309,310 The BMD data are similar to those associated with hormonal and alendronate therapy.

The Long-term Intervention on Fractures with Tibolone (LIFT) study was a randomized, placebo-controlled multicenter trial in 22 countries of tibolone, 1.25 mg, given daily over 3 years.311 The 4,538 women who participated in the trial were aged 60 to 85, all at high risk of fractures because of osteoporosis, and all treated with calcium and vitamin D supplementation. The study was stopped in February 2006 after a mean treatment of 34 months because of an increased risk of stroke. The risks of all events were assessed after 5 years of follow-up. The reduction of fractures was about 4 times as great in women who already had a vertebral fracture upon entry to the study compared with women who had not had a fracture at baseline. It is noteworthy that the number of falls in the treated

group was 25% less. The increase in stroke was greater in the oldest women (over age 70).

Based on previous bone density studies, the results of the LIFT trial on fracture reduction were not unexpected. The magnitude of the effect is roughly comparable to those with estrogen, bisphosphonates, and raloxifene (with the important exception being a lack of effect of raloxifene on hip fractures). A reduction of breast cancer was comparable to that reported with tamoxifen and raloxifene, but this was not a primary endpoint of the study.

The reported risk of stroke is similar to that observed with estrogen. In the WHI, no increase in stroke was observed in women under age 60 who had an absence of stroke risk factors. **It seems prudent to avoid the use of tibolone in older adult women and in women who are at risk for stroke** (specifically those with hypertension, smoking, diabetes, or atrial fibrillation). The Osteoporosis Prevention and Arterial effects of tibolone (OPAL) study was a 3-year, randomized, double-blind trial in six U.S. centers and five European centers, treating 866 postmenopausal women with 2.5 mg tibolone daily, 0.625/2.5 mg daily of conjugated estrogens/medroxyprogesterone acetate, or placebo.312 Unfortunately, the OPAL trial did not achieve its goal of providing robust data on cardiovascular effects, due to the older age of the women and the notably different results in American and European women. There continues to be good reason to believe that tibolone will have a neutral effect in terms of CHD.

In summary, tibolone treatment of postmenopausal women is as effective as estrogen therapy in relieving hot flushes, preventing bone loss, and increasing vaginal lubrication and stimulates libido to a greater degree than estrogen. There is less breast tenderness and mastalgia with tibolone. Endometrial safety has been reported to be comparable to that achieved with continuous combined estrogen-progestin regimens and with a lower rate of breakthrough bleeding. The standard dose of tibolone for many years was 2.5 mg daily, but the new studies support the use of the lower dose,

1.25 mg, with no apparent loss of bone efficacy. Tibolone continues to be an appropriate choice for hormonal therapy, suitable for many postmenopausal women.

**Parathyroid Hormone Analogues**

PTH increases bone formation. Teriparatide (Forteo) is the recombinant human 1 to 34 amino acid fragment of PTH. Another preparation encompasses the 1 to 84 sequence and has shown efficacy in vertebral and nonvertebral fracture risk reduction but is not available in the United States.313 Given to postmenopausal women with osteoporosis in a once-daily, subcutaneous dose of 20 mg, teriparatide produces a greater increase in bone density and possibly a greater reduction in fractures compared with estrogen or alendronate.314,315 Statistically significant reductions in both vertebral and nonvertebral fractures were observed in randomized controlled trials. In patients with osteoporosis, teriparatide can reduce the risk of vertebral fractures by 65% to 77%, and nonvertebral fractures by 35% to 53%, after an average of 18 months of treatment.316 **Because of the expense and difficulty in self-administration, treatment with the parathyroid fragment is best directed to individuals with severe osteoporosis and at high risk for fractures.** After a relatively short period of treatment, no more than 2 years, the gain in bone can be maintained with one of the antiresorptive agents.317-320 Forteo is currently available as a 20 μg daily subcutaneous injection and generic preparations are now available as the patent expired in 2019.321

Abaloparatide (Tymlos)is an analogue of parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP), which was approved by the FDA in April 2017 for the treatment of postmenopausal women with osteoporosis. The recommended dosage is 80 μg administered subcutaneously once daily. Both teriparatide and abaloparatide are effective in reducing the risk of new vertebral and nonvertebral fractures.321

***Romosozumab***

Romosozumab (Evenity) is a humanized monoclonal antibody (immunoglobulin G [IgG]2) produced in a mammalian cell line (Chinese hamster ovary) by recombinant DNA technology that binds to and inhibits sclerostin. Sclerostin is a regulatory protein secreted by the osteocytes, and in turn regulates and suppresses osteoblast bone formation by acting through a cell surface receptor on the osteoblasts; the net effect of sclerostin is inhibition of bone formation.322 Inhibition of sclerotin, therefore, is an

effective approach to stimulate bone formation and thereby achieve net gain in bone mass. Romosozumab is approved for the treatment of osteoporosis in postmenopausal women at high risk for fracture, or for those who have failed or cannot tolerate other available osteoporosis treatment. It is available as a subcutaneous monthly injection, and recommended duration of use is limited to 12 months, beyond which an alternative regimen should be substituted. Romosozumab and PTH analogues are the only two classes of **anabolic agents** available for those at a significantly high risk for fracture (such as those with existing/multiple fractures). The anabolic effect gets attenuated beyond 12 months use and hence switching to an alternative antiresorptive regimen is recommended. In the Fracture Study in Postmenopausal Women with Osteoporosis (FRAME) trial, romosozumab treatment for 12 months resulted in reduced risk of new vertebral fracture by 73% and clinical fractures by 36% compared to placebo.323

### Alternative Therapies to Prevent Bone Loss

Phytoestrogens are effective in preventing bone loss in rats, but not in monkeys.324-326 In women, some studies demonstrated at best a slight effect on spinal bone, but most found no benefit in either the spine or hip.87,327-329 Flaxseed supplementation had no effect on biomarkers of bone metabolism.330 The difference between hip fracture incidence in Japanese and American women may be due to structural and/or genetic differences, not dietary intake of soy.331

Ipriflavone is a synthetic isoflavone which gets metabolized to daidzein. Studies with ipriflavone demonstrated prevention of bone loss over a year.332-335 Overall, the effect on bone was not as great as that observed with a standard dose of estrogen, or alendronate, perhaps not great enough to yield a benefit. A 4-year randomized trial in Europe assessed the effect of ipriflavone on bone density, urinary markers, and vertebral fractures in 474 women and could find no difference in the treated group compared with the placebo group.336

Equol is a bacterial metabolite, and the only hormonally active metabolite, of the soy phytoestrogen, daidzein. At least in vitro, equol stimulates gene transcription with both estrogen receptors and with a greater potency than any other isoflavone.337 Equol formation is totally

dependent on intestinal microflora. The most important observation regarding equol is that most adults do not produce equol, even when challenged with high doses of soy.338 This is a contrast to nonhuman primates and other animals; all that have been studied produce high levels of equol. Thus, there are two human populations: equol producers and nonequol producers. The key question is whether equol producers receive greater clinical effects from phytoestrogens than nonequol producers. As noted, thus far the clinical effects of isoflavones on bone have not been impressive. In a 2-year randomized trial of postmenopausal women, isoflavone-rich soy milk increased spinal bone mass in the 45% of the subjects who were equol producers, with essentially no effect in nonequol producers.338 Similar results were obtained in a Japanese trial testing the effect of isoflavone treatment in equol producers and nonproducers.339 Therefore, the population destined to receive a benefit from soy intake may be limited to equol producers. Studies need to be repeated measuring the responses in individuals who are identified as equol producers or nonequol producers. If the population destined to receive a benefit from soy intake is limited to equol producers, a convenient, inexpensive method must be developed to identify equol production. It may be possible to convert nonproducers to producers. A more straightforward approach is to administer equol itself. Daidzein yields two forms in equol producers, the *R*-equol inactive isomer and *S*-equol, the active isomer that binds to estrogen receptor-β. *S*-equol has been synthesized and its administration is effective for the treatment of menopausal symptoms.340 Another alternative is the *S*-equol supplement made by incubating equol-producing bacteria with soy isoflavones.341,342 It is absorbed readily with high bioavailability; low doses must be administered twice daily because of a relatively short half-life.

**Statins**

Statin treatment is, of course, a mainstay in programs designed to prevent cardiovascular disease. Prompted by animal experiments indicating that statins increase bone formation, case-control studies reported that statin treatment was associated with a reduced risk of fracture.343,344 However, analysis of a randomized trial of statin therapy and cardiovascular disease

failed to detect an effect on fracture risk, and a cohort study reported that an apparent beneficial effect of statin on fracture risk was influenced by various confounding factors.345,346 In the prospective cohort arm of the WHI, bone densities and fracture rates were similar when comparing statin users with nonusers.347 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis assessed the efficacy of statins on bone density, on markers of bone turnover, and on fracture risk by examining data from 33 clinical trials.348 Statin use was associated with a decreased risk of overall fractures (odds ratio [OR] = 0.81; 95% CI = 0.73-0.89) and hip fractures (OR = 0.75; 95% CI = 0.60-

0.92). Statin use was associated with statistically significantly higher bone density parameters at the total hip and the lumbar spine and with improved bone formation marker, osteocalcin. Interestingly, skeletal benefits of statins demonstrated a gender differential being more robust in males compared to female users. **Based on existing evidence, statin users should be reassured regarding the potential for skeletal benefit, but statins should not replace proven effective medications to prevent or treat osteoporosis.**

**Thiazide Diuretics**

Older women are often treated with thiazides for hypertension. Thiazides reduce the urinary loss of calcium and induce a positive calcium balance, and treatment is associated with a higher bone density. It is useful to know that estrogen and thiazides are additive; a significantly higher bone density is achieved with combined use.349 A recent meta-analysis that included 2,193,160 participants from 11 cohort studies evaluated the relationship between thiazide diuretic use and fracture. Users of thiazide diuretics experienced a statistically significant 14% reduction in the risk of all fractures and 18% reduction in the risk of hip fracture compared to nonusers.350 **Based on existing evidence, users of thiazide diuretics should be reassured regarding the potential for skeletal benefit, but thiazides should not replace proven effective medications to prevent or treat osteoporosis.**

**Lifestyle Modifications**

Lifestyle can have a beneficial effect on bone density as well as reduce fracture risk. Physical activity (weight-bearing), as little as 30 minutes a day for 3 days a week, will increase the mineral content of bone in older women.351 To be effective, exercise must exert a load on bone, especially the spine.352 Ordinary walking, while beneficial for balance and for overall health, alone will not suffice for improving bone density.353 Even brisk walking achieves a significant increase in bone density only in the calcaneus, the site subjected to stress with walking.354 However, brisk walking may slow the rate of bone loss in the hip.355 In other words, weight lifting is better for the spine than is ordinary walking, although running probably helps hip bone mass. The activities that are beneficial are running, weight training, aerobics, stair climbing, and sports other than swimming. The effect of weight-bearing exercise on bone density is additive when combined with hormone therapy.356 Although ordinary walking has little impact on bone density, it is still reasonable to expect walking to have an overall beneficial effect on the risk of fracture. Walking improves the cardiovascular status of patients and reduces body mass. These changes plus the exercise itself improve balance and decrease the risk of falling. For these reasons, walking, even after adjusting for bone density and body weight, is associated with a reduced risk of hip fracture.94,357

The impact of exercise on bone is significantly less than that achieved by hormone therapy.188 Women require the full combination of pharmacologic therapy, calcium and vitamin D supplementation, and exercise in order to minimize the risk of fractures. For each of these, the beneficial impact lasts only as long as the therapy is continued.

**Cigarette smoking and excessive alcohol consumption are associated with an increased risk of osteoporosis.** The magnitude of bone loss associated with cigarette smoking is consistent with a 40% to 45% increase in the risk of hip fracture.358 Women who smoke also enter menopause earlier and lose bone at a greater rate in the first years of the postmenopausal period.359

A high coffee intake has been reported to be associated with an increased risk of osteoporosis.360 However, this increase in risk is dependent on dietary calcium intake. In women who drank at least one glass of milk (300 mg calcium) per day throughout most of their lives, increasing caffeinated coffee intake was not associated with a lower bone density.361

Repeatedly, we see the importance of teaching children and adolescents the merits of an adequate calcium intake; drinking nonfat milk throughout life is good for you. An adequate calcium intake compensates for “calcium robbers,” such as caffeine and soft drinks. A British study concluded that an increase of only 300 mL/d of milk in adolescents increased bone density without an increase in weight or body fat.194

**Remember that not all fractures are solely due to osteoporosis.** Drug side effects, impaired vision, neurologic dysfunction, and muscular conditions all put patients at risk because more than 90% of fractures occur following a fall38 (**Figure 23.13**).

Interventions that reduce the odds of falling and enhance the ability to withstand the impact of a fall are important.362 This includes patient education regarding hazards in the home, monitoring drug use, adequate nutrition, and a good exercise program. In addition, there is evidence that estrogen with or without added progestin improves muscle strength and balance.363-368 On the other hand, some studies have not been able to document an increase in muscle strength or improvements in balance.356,369-

372 Furthermore, the increase in muscle mass and strength in response to weight-bearing exercise was the same when hormone users were compared with nonusers.373 Nevertheless, a cohort study reported 71% fewer falls in early postmenopausal hormone users and 43% in late postmenopausal hormone users.374 Use of hip guards has been shown to reduce the risk of hip fractures in institutionalized older adult, but not in ambulant populations.375

### Bone-Losing Medications

Clinicians should always remember that exposure to excessive thyroid and glucocorticoid hormones is associated with osteoporosis and an increased rate of fractures. The bone loss associated with glucocorticoid treatment is significantly prevented by hormone or bisphosphonate therapy.376-379 **Excessive thyroid effects can be avoided by periodic (at least annually) monitoring of treatment dosage with TSH levels.** Specific preventive treatment should also be offered to patients using long-term anticonvulsants or heparin.

Considerable publicity has successfully raised clinical consciousness regarding the increased risk of fractures associated with the use of corticosteroids. We should similarly recognize the increased risk of fractures with the daily use of SSRIs. The Canadian Multicentre Osteoporosis Study Research Group reported the effect of daily SSRIs in a prospective cohort study in seven regional centers of 5,008 adults over the age of 50.78 After adjusting for age, hip bone density, fractures at baseline, and estrogen use in women, daily SSRI use was associated with a 2-fold increased risk of fragility fractures. Daily use of SSRIs was also associated with about a 2-fold increased risk of falling, and these individuals had lower bone densities. Controlling for falls and lower bone density still left an

increased risk of fractures in SSRI users that began after 1 to 1.5 years of use.

Does this side effect of SSRIs make sense? Is it the SSRI or the lifestyle associated with clinical depression? Serotonin does not cross the blood-brain barrier; in the brain, serotonin activity is the result of synthesis, reuptake, and binding to a 5-hydroxytryptophan receptor, which is inhibited by SSRIs. Most of the circulating serotonin comes from synthesis in the duodenum by specialized neuroendocrine cells. Locally released serotonin stimulates intestinal peristalsis, whereas the serotonin entering the circulation is taken up by platelets or serotonin can reach a target tissue like bone, linking bone formation to the gut. Mice with a mutation for the serotonin transporter protein develop less bone mass and strength.79 Serotonin receptors and serotonin transport have been identified in osteoblasts and osteocytes. The bone effects of PTH and mechanical stimulation are modulated by the serotonin system. Therefore, daily SSRI use can impair bone formation, tilting the balance in favor of resorption and bone loss, and decreased bone densities have been reported in both male and female SSRI users (but not in users of tricyclic antidepressants).80,81

It is not always easy to know which came first, depression or fractures, leading to subsequent depression. It has been reported that depressed people and SSRI users have a greater incidence of falls,73 and thus it is not unreasonable to consider that depression comes first in some people. However, orthostatic hypotension and syncope are more common in SSRI users, and this could also contribute to the greater prevalence of falls.

Depressed people are sedentary and eat poorly, factors that favor bone loss. Some have speculated that increased cortisol levels associated with depression might lead to bone loss, similar to that observed with the pharmacologic administration of corticosteroids. On the other hand, American studies, despite finding a link between depression and fractures, failed to detect an increase in depression associated with lower bone density measurements.72,73 However, other studies have reported increases in depression associated with lower bone densities.74-76

Obviously, this is an unsettled picture, but we should be more aware of the possible increased risk of fractures with the daily use of SSRIs. Interventions that reduce the odds of falling and enhance the ability to withstand the impact of a fall are important. This includes patient education

regarding hazards in the home, monitoring drug use, adequate nutrition, and a good exercise program. Aggressive monitoring of bone density is warranted; adequate calcium and vitamin D supplementation are necessary, and until more studies clarify this problem, it seems reasonable to consider treatment with one of the antiresorptive agents.

Proton pump-inhibiting drugs used to treat gastroesophageal reflux impair intestinal calcium absorption, resulting in secondary hyperparathyroidism and bone loss. Long-term use of omeprazole has been linked to an increase in hip fractures in case-control studies.380,381 These drugs should be added to the list of bone-losing medications that require careful monitoring of bone density and antiresorptive treatment when indicated. The list includes, of course, any treatment that produces a hypoestrogenic state, for example, an aromatase inhibitor.

Thiazolidinediones used to treat diabetes are associated with an increase in bone loss and fractures.382,383 Bone density measurements and careful assessment for fracture risk are important components of health care for women who are diabetic, with emphasis on adequate calcium and vitamin D supplementation and appropriate treatment with one of the antiresorptive agents where indicated.

### Management of a Nonresponder

**Response to treatment is assessed by repeating BMD assessment.** Treatment efficacy is reflected in stable or improved BMD repeated at a minimum interval of 1 year from the baseline study. **It is important to appreciate that the number to focus on when assessing interval change is of the actual BMD that is reported in g/cm2, and not the *T*-scores.** There are two reasons to question the prevalence of nonresponse to treatment. The first reason is the argument that the results of single bone density measurements display the phenomenon of regression to the mean (extreme results are in part due to random error). Analysis of the hip and spinal bone density data from the randomized clinical trials assessing the effects of alendronate and raloxifene in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis revealed that women who lost BMD after 1 year of treatment were likely to gain bone during the second year.384 The more extreme the measurement after 1 year of treatment, the more likely the next year’s measurement indicated a reversal. Therefore, it has been recommended that bone treatments should not be discontinued when measurements after 1 year indicate loss of bone density because repeat results are usually closer to the mean. Once a lack of response is identified based on repeat bone density assessment, the next step should be to inquire about compliance, particularly with prescribed oral agents, and assess serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D and calcium levels as vitamin D deficiency is a common mechanism for failure of antifracture treatments. Optimal vitamin D levels are necessary to maximize the response to any antiresorption agent.385 This emphasizes the need to titer the dose of vitamin D supplementation by measuring the circulating level of 25-hydroxyvitamin D. A value less than 30 ng/mL is below normal; less than 20 ng/mL is a definitive indication of vitamin D deficiency. Assessment of bone turnover markers can allow insights into compliance as well as effectiveness as a minimum of 30% reduction in markers of bone resorption should occur with the use of estrogens, raloxifene, bisphosphonates, and denosumab.

A minimal time interval of 1 y is needed to allow detection of any meaningful change in bone density.

**Assessing Blood Estrogen Levels in Hormone Users**

On average, about 10% to 15% of women may lose bone despite being prescribed hormone therapy. **Because of the precision error with bone density measurements, a change of at least 3% to 5% is necessary to be clinically significant.** A Finnish 5-year clinical trial reported a prevalence of poor response based on bone density of 11% for spinal bone and 26% for the hip.386 As expected, smoking and low body weight were common findings among the poor responders, but the most impressive characteristics were lower estradiol and higher FSH levels. It is only logical that there exists a group of women who metabolize and clear administered estrogens at a greater rate and thus require a higher dose to sustain a protective effect on bone. Indeed, considerable variation in estradiol levels has been documented in individuals receiving both oral and transdermal hormone therapies.387,388 Marketing presentations by the pharmaceutical companies provide mean levels, suggesting stable and smooth maintenance of blood levels; however, the ranges, which are wide, are not revealed. An aim of individualizing hormone therapy is to determine the appropriate dose for the intended objective; in the case of bone, antiresorptive effects are

apparent when serum estradiol levels exceed 20 pg/mL and are optimized with estradiol in the 40 to 60 pg/mL range. In patients using hormone therapy for osteoporosis prevention or management, if repeat bone density assessment demonstrates evidence of ongoing bone loss, proceeding with assessing serum estradiol level is an appropriate next step. A practical range for a blood sample derived during office hours from a patient taking her medication at night is 50 to 100 pg/mL. To minimize variation, we encourage clinicians to always use the same laboratory, after first establishing the values obtained using standard doses of estradiol in their own practices. The reliability of blood estrogen measurements is questionable when conjugated equine estrogens (CEEs) are the treatment being used, although some assays can provide reproducible results because of cross-reactions with the specific antibody in the assay.

As clinicians and the pharmaceutical industry promote lower doses of estrogen in order to minimize estrogen-related risks, a greater rate of poor response as measured by bone density can be expected. For otherwise healthy women without significant risk for cerebrovascular and cardiovascular harm, uptitration of estrogen dose can be safely considered if evidence of lack of skeletal response is encountered. Safety of such an approach can be ensured by monitoring of serum concentrations of estradiol. We would further emphasize that appropriate detection and investigation will confirm that a relatively low blood estradiol level is the cause of most cases of poor bone response to hormone treatment. An acidic vaginal pH (<4.5) correlates with an appropriate dose of estrogen administration and is a very simple and inexpensive way of assessing adequacy of dosing.389,390

## CONCLUSION

Osteoporosis, especially in the postmenopausal setting, poses unique clinical and health maintenance challenges. The diminishing of BMD leading to a fracture can have profound effects not only on quality of life but also on a woman’s risk of death. Hence diligent screening and consistent calcium and vitamin supplementation are crucial in sustaining or, at least, minimizing deficits in BMD. When this prevention is not enough to diminish bone deterioration, then there is a broad arsenal of antiresorptive and anabolic medications available to either pause bone density loss or even reverse it. By adequately preventing, detecting, and treating osteoporosis, a patient’s well-being can be maintained and even improved long after the menopausal transition and sustained well into decades postmenopause.
