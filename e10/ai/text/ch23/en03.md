***How Long Should Bisphosphonate Treatment Be Continued?***

In an extension of the alendronate Fracture Intervention Trial (FIT), the participants were randomized after 5 years of treatment either to another 5 years of treatment or placebo.267 The group that discontinued alendronate treatment (5 or 10 mg/d) experienced small losses of BMD over 5 years. The levels, however, remained above the pretreatment levels 10 years previously. There were no differences in nonvertebral fractures between the treatment and placebo groups, but there was a 2-fold higher rate of clinically recognizable vertebral fractures in the placebo group (5.3% vs 2.4%). It was concluded that for most women, a period of drug holiday may be safely considered after 5 years of treatment with alendronate and that long-term treatment beyond 5 years should be limited to high-risk women (women with existing vertebral fractures or very low bone densities).

The rate of hip fracture among women who discontinued bisphosphonate therapy was compared with women who remained on treatment.268 The study group consisted of 9,063 users who had been compliant for at least 2 years. The hip fracture rate almost doubled in the women who discontinued treatment compared with those who did not. After discontinuation for a year or longer, the risk of hip fracture increased by 9 months in women with low compliance rates. In women with high

compliance rates for 2 or 3 years, there were no significant differences in fracture risk after discontinuation for up to 1 year later. Why is there this lingering effect?

The unique tight binding of bisphosphonates to bone matrix causes this class of drugs to remain in the body for decades. This is believed to be the explanation for why there is no rapid bone loss after discontinuing bisphosphonate treatment in contrast to the rapid loss that follows the termination of treatments such as estrogen or raloxifene or denosumab (discussed later). This is also the reason why concerns have been raised regarding long-term treatment because when bone remodeling releases bound bisphosphonate, it is free to be active again, and as a result, the endogenous bisphosphonate is added to the administered bisphosphonate, raising dosage exposure. The potential risk that has been long recognized is that prolonged exposure to bisphosphonates or excessive dosage would oversuppress bone resorption, thus oversuppressing bone turnover, a phenomenon referred to as *adynamic* bone that ultimately affects the biomechanical strength of bone and allows microcracks to accumulate. A unique type of fracture of the femur, commonly referred to as “atypical fracture,” is characterized by a transverse fracture in an area marked by hypertrophy of the cortex and has been associated with long-term use of bisphosphonates.269

***Bisphosphonates and Osteonecrosis of the Jaw***

Osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ) is a rare complication that has been described with long-term use of potent antiresorptive agents, including bisphosphonates and denosumab.270 In an analysis of information from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) databank linked to Medicare claims, 16,703 patients with cancer were identified who were treated with intravenous (IV) bisphosphonates (pamidronate and zoledronic acid) from 1995 to 2003.271 When 14,349 treated patients were matched with 28,698 controls, the treated group had a 3-fold increased risk of jaw or facial bone surgery and a very large increased risk of osteomyelitis of the jaw. The estimated absolute risk equaled 5.48 events per 100 patients over 6 years. In addition, the risk increased with increasing cumulative dose. The absolute risk is much less in patients without cancer being treated with bisphosphonates for the management of osteoporosis, in whom the

incidence of ONJ is estimated at 0.001% to 0.01%, minimally higher than in the general population.270

The mechanism underlying ONJ in bisphosphonate users is uncertain beyond the recognition that infection and blood flow changes are involved. It is postulated that a compromised healing ability of the bone because of inhibition of bone turnover leads to sequestered osteomyelitis and necrosis. Studies have suggested that the risk of ONJ is greater in patients treated with zoledronic acid compared with pamidronate.272-274 Awareness of this problem has led to increased attention to oral hygiene and the avoidance of tooth extractions in the high-risk population of patients with cancer receiving bisphosphonate treatment.

Treatment related risks of osteomyelitis and ONJ have long been recognized, although controversy remains regarding a causative role of bisphosphonates for these risks. To be sure, most of the cases have been in patients with cancer, usually treated with IV high doses, but this complication was also reported in patients receiving oral bisphosphonate treatment for osteoporosis, with no history or evidence of malignancy.274,275 Experts in the field point out that many clinicians specializing in osteoporosis have never seen a case. **The incidence of ONJ with oral bisphosphonates is somewhere from 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 100,000.** They further argue that a true cause-and-effect relationship would require appropriate controlled studies, and two are ongoing. But clinical decision-making cannot be delayed until data are available, and it is by no means certain that the ongoing trials will yield definitive results on such a rare event. Patients should be cautioned regarding this rare problem and urged to practice good dental care. **We recommend consideration for dental evaluation and cleaning for patients in whom initiation of bisphosphonates for management of osteoporosis is being considered, prior to initiation of therapy, attention to oral hygiene during the course of treatment, and ongoing periodical surveillance.**276

***Bisphosphonates and Atrial Fibrillation***

An increase in the incidence of new-onset atrial fibrillation has been linked with the use of bisphosphonates, more so with IV than oral regimens.277 A meta-analysis presented at the meeting of the American College of Chest Physicians in October 2008 estimated that atrial fibrillation is observed in

2.5% to 3% of individuals treated with bisphosphonates and that 1% to 2% experience hospitalization or death. A case-control study concluded atrial fibrillation could be attributed to bisphosphonates in 3% of previous or current users.278 These positive reports were followed by a series of consistently negative studies. A larger Danish case-control study concluded that there was no link between atrial fibrillation and the use of bisphosphonates.279 A Danish cohort study of patients with fracture found no increase in the risks of stroke or myocardial infarction and concluded that an increase in atrial fibrillation could be attributed to the use of bisphosphonates in individuals already at increased risk for cardiovascular events.280 In two large American cohort databases of patients undergoing coronary angiography, there was no increase in atrial fibrillation or myocardial infarction associated with bisphosphonate treatment.281 A retrospective cohort study from Taiwan compared bisphosphonate use with the use of raloxifene and reported a similar rate of atrial fibrillation in both treatment groups.282 An apparent increase in atrial fibrillation can be attributed to the fact that most bisphosphonate users are older, have more cardiovascular disease, and are at greater risk for atrial fibrillation. Nonetheless, caution is advised in the older adult and those at risk, such as those with known hypertensive disorders, ischemic heart disease, cardiomyopathy, and valvular heart disease.

***Bisphosphonates and Esophageal Cancer***

In a Letter to the Editor in the January 1, 2009, issue of the *New England Journal of Medicine*, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) reported 23 cases (8 fatal) of esophageal cancer in patients being treated with alendronate.283 As of 2009, a total of 31 cases of esophageal cancer had been collected in Europe and Japan associated with alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and etidronate. Although this is a small number of cases with drugs that have been used for more than a decade by millions of people, the concern has added credibility because of the well-recognized side effect of esophageal injury with oral bisphosphonates. Nevertheless, the FDA report was anecdotal in nature, with no comparison group. In response, American and European comparisons of treated patients to the

U.S. expected incidence of esophageal cancer or to nontreated patients in national databases in the United Kingdom and Denmark failed to detect an

increase in esophageal cancer in bisphosphonate-treated patients.284,285 A meta-analysis of 11 studies published in 2015 was similarly reassuring and failed to identify any evidence to support an increase in risk of upper gastrointestinal cancers with the use of bisphosphonates.286

***Bisphosphonates and Pain***

The FDA has informed clinicians that patients taking bisphosphonates rarely experience severe bone, joint, or muscle pain that is relieved when treatment stops; discontinuation of bisphosphonate treatment and consideration for an alternative agent are advised in this setting.

Bisphosphonate treatment is best reserved for older postmenopausal women. It is not a drug class of choice for the prevention of osteoporosis in relatively young postmenopausal women in whom hormone therapy may be preferentially considered as a first-line approach for osteoporosis prevention and fracture risk reduction.

In all except very-high-risk patients being treated with bisphosphonates, it would be wise to consider a time limit for the duration of exposure. Bone density should be measured after 2 to 4 years of treatment, and a period of “drug holiday” should be considered after 5 years of use for those who are not deemed at a markedly high fracture risk. Patients should be followed by monitoring bone density, with a resumption of treatment in those who rapidly lose bone or in those who accumulate a loss of 5% to 10% in 1 year.

The onset of severe pain at any site is an indication to discontinue bisphosphonate treatment.

In the absence of demonstrated fracture reduction benefit, the increased cost and likely increase in adverse events from a combination regimen (such as estrogen and bisphosphonate), such an approach is not currently recommended.

Key Points: Treatment With Bisphosphonates

**Denosumab**

Denosumab (Prolia) is a human monoclonal antibody to RANKL and is one of the newest of the available antifracture agents that was approved for the

treatment of osteoporosis in high-risk patients. RANKL is secreted by osteoblasts and binds to its receptor, RANK, on the surface of osteoclasts, stimulating the osteoclasts to mature and to resorb bone (**Figure 23.2**). OPG is a soluble receptor that is produced by the osteoblasts and that acts as a sponge to bind circulating RANKL, thereby preventing the RANK-mediated activation of osteoclasts; in the absence of RANK signaling, osteoclasts undergo apoptosis. An agent that could act like OPG, therefore, would prevent bone loss. Denosumab works like OPG, but rather than competing with RANK for its activator RANKL, it binds with great affinity to RANKL, thus preventing activation of RANK. The antiresorptive effects of denosumab are thus distinct from those of bisphosphonates and estrogens. Denosumab prevents both maturation and survival of osteoclasts and active bone resorption by osteoclasts.

The impact of denosumab on bone density has been evaluated in phase 2 clinical trials.287 The participants were postmenopausal women up to age 80 who had osteoporosis based on low *T*-scores. For the first 2 years, the patients were randomized to one of seven denosumab doses, alendronate, or placebo. After 2 years, patients in five of the treated groups were continued for 2 more years on 60 mg daily given subcutaneously only once every 6 months. One treatment arm was discontinued, and then after a year, treatment was resumed with 60 mg denosumab every 6 months. One treatment arm was discontinued after 2 years and followed. Two hundred and sixty-two women completed the study. Long-term treatment increased bone density in the spine and hip, whereas the placebo group continued to lose bone density. A reduction in markers for bone turnover was sustained. The group that discontinued treatment after 2 years lost bone density; after a year, retreatment with denosumab restored the bone to the level of the gain achieved in the first 2 years, levels similar to the groups who continued treatment for 4 years. No significant differences in the treatment and placebo groups were noted for adverse events. Similar results have been reported in a phase 3 trial with 332 women in 21 centers in Canada and the United States.288 In a large, 3-year, worldwide multicenter trial involving 7,868 women with osteoporosis, the Fracture Reduction Evaluation of Denosumab in Osteoporosis Every 6 Months (FREEDOM) trial, treatment with 60 mg denosumab subcutaneously every 6 months reduced vertebral fractures by 68%, hip fractures by 40%, and other nonvertebral fractures by

20%.289 A separate phase 3 trial concluded that denosumab treatment increased BMD at the hip and spine significantly more than alendronate.290 There were no differences in major adverse events in these trials, including osteonecrosis, or indications of suppressed immune function; however, eczema and cellulitis were significantly increased in the worldwide study.289 The data representing 10 years of denosumab exposure (3 years of exposure in the FREEDOM trial plus 7-year exposure in the open-label extension study) offer reassurance regarding safety and efficacy of long-term use. Not only did the incidence of fractures in the long-term users remain comparable to that observed during the FREEDOM trial; additionally, there was evidence of ongoing improvement in BMD during the 10-year period of exposure. Adverse event profile was similarly comparable to that encountered in the 3-year FREEDOM trial.291

How do the results compare with bisphosphonates? The increase in bone density at the spine and hip is greater with denosumab, and total body bone density, as exemplified by the radius, increases with denosumab, but not with bisphosphonates. **After discontinuation of denosumab, however, bone loss begins immediately and the concept of drug holiday does not hold for denosumab. Prescribing information warns of the possibility of an increased risk of multiple vertebral fractures following discontinuation.**292 This quick reversibility contrasts with bisphosphonates that bind tightly to bone, remaining in bone for decades and maintaining a long-term effect. The 6-month dosing regimen of denosumab and subcutaneous route of administration offer convenience. Alternatively, the requirement for subcutaneous administration might seem to be an obstacle to some, but on the other hand, a required visit to the office might increase continuation rates. **Given that data on safety and ongoing efficacy of denosumab beyond 10 years of use are wanting, in our opinion, it is prudent to then consider switching to a bisphosphonate to ensure that the skeletal benefit gained from denosumab use persists beyond its discontinuation.**

**Calcitonin**

Calcitonin regulates plasma calcium by inhibiting bone resorption and has been long recognized to offer fracture risk reduction. The antifracture

efficacy of calcitonin is inferior to the agents discussed earlier. In a randomized 5-year trial, calcitonin treatment reduced spinal fractures with less of an effect (about 33%) compared with estrogen, alendronate, and raloxifene and had no reduction in hip fractures.293 Studies with intranasal delivery of salmon calcitonin (200 IU daily) indicate it can increase bone density. Human calcitonin is available, but recombinant salmon calcitonin (Fortical and Miacalcin) is more potent. Calcitonin treatment should be combined with vitamin D and calcium supplementation. The lack of a dose-response and a high dropout rate raised questions regarding the potency and efficacy of calcitonin in preventing fractures. Calcitonin, however, still has a place for use in patients who are unable to tolerate alternative agents, as well as for patients with intractable back pain, given its analgesic potential.294

**Fluoride**

The addition of fluoride, a potent stimulator of bone formation, can offer significant protection against osteoporosis. The clinical response depends on the formulation and dose. Slow-release sodium fluoride (25 mg bid given 12 of every 14 months) combined with calcium supplementation reduced the vertebral fracture rate with essentially no side effects.295 Treatment is recommended for no longer than 4 years to avoid the toxic accumulation of fluoride in bone.296 This treatment is reserved for patients with established postmenopausal osteoporosis; however, a commercial preparation has not yet been approved.

**Strontium**

A divalent strontium salt of ranelic acid acts through uncoupling and rebalancing of the bone turnover in favor of net bone formation. A dose of 2 g of strontium ranelate is dissolved in water and taken at bedtime. Clinical trials indicate that a reduction in spinal and nonvertebral fractures is achieved comparable to antiresorptive agents.297,298 Antifracture efficacy has been reported with up to 8 years of use. The main side effect is nausea and diarrhea that usually resolves in a few months. In pooled analyses, an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) was associated with strontium ranelate relative to placebo over 5 years of treatment, and

ongoing caution is advised. Strontium is not approved for the management of osteoporosis in the United States, but oral strontium ranelate is available in many countries (Protelos, Protos, Protaxos, Bivalos, Osseor) for use in the management of osteoporosis.299

**Tibolone**

Tibolone is structurally related to the 19-nortestosterone progestins used clinically in oral contraceptives. Its chemistry, mechanisms of action, and clinical effects were discussed in detail earlier in this chapter. Tibolone prevents bone loss in postmenopausal women as effectively as estrogen or estrogen-progestin therapy.300-306 In a large U.S. dose-response study with doses ranging from 0.3 to 2.5 mg daily, only the 1.25- and 2.5-mg doses produced progressive bone density increases in the femoral neck. Indeed, the impact on bone was essentially the same for the two highest doses, 1.25 and 2.5 mg. Although the 1.25-mg dose is acceptable for the prevention of bone loss, the 2.5-mg dose is more effective for the alleviation of hot flushes.307

The beneficial impact on bone can be attributed to tibolone’s estrogenic metabolites acting through the estrogen receptor because it is blocked by an antiestrogen, but not by an antiandrogen or an antiprogestin.308 Tibolone prevents the bone loss associated with gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist treatment (and the side effect of hot flushing).309,310 The BMD data are similar to those associated with hormonal and alendronate therapy.

The Long-term Intervention on Fractures with Tibolone (LIFT) study was a randomized, placebo-controlled multicenter trial in 22 countries of tibolone, 1.25 mg, given daily over 3 years.311 The 4,538 women who participated in the trial were aged 60 to 85, all at high risk of fractures because of osteoporosis, and all treated with calcium and vitamin D supplementation. The study was stopped in February 2006 after a mean treatment of 34 months because of an increased risk of stroke. The risks of all events were assessed after 5 years of follow-up. The reduction of fractures was about 4 times as great in women who already had a vertebral fracture upon entry to the study compared with women who had not had a fracture at baseline. It is noteworthy that the number of falls in the treated

group was 25% less. The increase in stroke was greater in the oldest women (over age 70).

Based on previous bone density studies, the results of the LIFT trial on fracture reduction were not unexpected. The magnitude of the effect is roughly comparable to those with estrogen, bisphosphonates, and raloxifene (with the important exception being a lack of effect of raloxifene on hip fractures). A reduction of breast cancer was comparable to that reported with tamoxifen and raloxifene, but this was not a primary endpoint of the study.

The reported risk of stroke is similar to that observed with estrogen. In the WHI, no increase in stroke was observed in women under age 60 who had an absence of stroke risk factors. **It seems prudent to avoid the use of tibolone in older adult women and in women who are at risk for stroke** (specifically those with hypertension, smoking, diabetes, or atrial fibrillation). The Osteoporosis Prevention and Arterial effects of tibolone (OPAL) study was a 3-year, randomized, double-blind trial in six U.S. centers and five European centers, treating 866 postmenopausal women with 2.5 mg tibolone daily, 0.625/2.5 mg daily of conjugated estrogens/medroxyprogesterone acetate, or placebo.312 Unfortunately, the OPAL trial did not achieve its goal of providing robust data on cardiovascular effects, due to the older age of the women and the notably different results in American and European women. There continues to be good reason to believe that tibolone will have a neutral effect in terms of CHD.

In summary, tibolone treatment of postmenopausal women is as effective as estrogen therapy in relieving hot flushes, preventing bone loss, and increasing vaginal lubrication and stimulates libido to a greater degree than estrogen. There is less breast tenderness and mastalgia with tibolone. Endometrial safety has been reported to be comparable to that achieved with continuous combined estrogen-progestin regimens and with a lower rate of breakthrough bleeding. The standard dose of tibolone for many years was 2.5 mg daily, but the new studies support the use of the lower dose,

1.25 mg, with no apparent loss of bone efficacy. Tibolone continues to be an appropriate choice for hormonal therapy, suitable for many postmenopausal women.

**Parathyroid Hormone Analogues**

PTH increases bone formation. Teriparatide (Forteo) is the recombinant human 1 to 34 amino acid fragment of PTH. Another preparation encompasses the 1 to 84 sequence and has shown efficacy in vertebral and nonvertebral fracture risk reduction but is not available in the United States.313 Given to postmenopausal women with osteoporosis in a once-daily, subcutaneous dose of 20 mg, teriparatide produces a greater increase in bone density and possibly a greater reduction in fractures compared with estrogen or alendronate.314,315 Statistically significant reductions in both vertebral and nonvertebral fractures were observed in randomized controlled trials. In patients with osteoporosis, teriparatide can reduce the risk of vertebral fractures by 65% to 77%, and nonvertebral fractures by 35% to 53%, after an average of 18 months of treatment.316 **Because of the expense and difficulty in self-administration, treatment with the parathyroid fragment is best directed to individuals with severe osteoporosis and at high risk for fractures.** After a relatively short period of treatment, no more than 2 years, the gain in bone can be maintained with one of the antiresorptive agents.317-320 Forteo is currently available as a 20 μg daily subcutaneous injection and generic preparations are now available as the patent expired in 2019.321

Abaloparatide (Tymlos)is an analogue of parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP), which was approved by the FDA in April 2017 for the treatment of postmenopausal women with osteoporosis. The recommended dosage is 80 μg administered subcutaneously once daily. Both teriparatide and abaloparatide are effective in reducing the risk of new vertebral and nonvertebral fractures.321

***Romosozumab***

Romosozumab (Evenity) is a humanized monoclonal antibody (immunoglobulin G [IgG]2) produced in a mammalian cell line (Chinese hamster ovary) by recombinant DNA technology that binds to and inhibits sclerostin. Sclerostin is a regulatory protein secreted by the osteocytes, and in turn regulates and suppresses osteoblast bone formation by acting through a cell surface receptor on the osteoblasts; the net effect of sclerostin is inhibition of bone formation.322 Inhibition of sclerotin, therefore, is an

effective approach to stimulate bone formation and thereby achieve net gain in bone mass. Romosozumab is approved for the treatment of osteoporosis in postmenopausal women at high risk for fracture, or for those who have failed or cannot tolerate other available osteoporosis treatment. It is available as a subcutaneous monthly injection, and recommended duration of use is limited to 12 months, beyond which an alternative regimen should be substituted. Romosozumab and PTH analogues are the only two classes of **anabolic agents** available for those at a significantly high risk for fracture (such as those with existing/multiple fractures). The anabolic effect gets attenuated beyond 12 months use and hence switching to an alternative antiresorptive regimen is recommended. In the Fracture Study in Postmenopausal Women with Osteoporosis (FRAME) trial, romosozumab treatment for 12 months resulted in reduced risk of new vertebral fracture by 73% and clinical fractures by 36% compared to placebo.323

### Alternative Therapies to Prevent Bone Loss

Phytoestrogens are effective in preventing bone loss in rats, but not in monkeys.324-326 In women, some studies demonstrated at best a slight effect on spinal bone, but most found no benefit in either the spine or hip.87,327-329 Flaxseed supplementation had no effect on biomarkers of bone metabolism.330 The difference between hip fracture incidence in Japanese and American women may be due to structural and/or genetic differences, not dietary intake of soy.331

Ipriflavone is a synthetic isoflavone which gets metabolized to daidzein. Studies with ipriflavone demonstrated prevention of bone loss over a year.332-335 Overall, the effect on bone was not as great as that observed with a standard dose of estrogen, or alendronate, perhaps not great enough to yield a benefit. A 4-year randomized trial in Europe assessed the effect of ipriflavone on bone density, urinary markers, and vertebral fractures in 474 women and could find no difference in the treated group compared with the placebo group.336

Equol is a bacterial metabolite, and the only hormonally active metabolite, of the soy phytoestrogen, daidzein. At least in vitro, equol stimulates gene transcription with both estrogen receptors and with a greater potency than any other isoflavone.337 Equol formation is totally

dependent on intestinal microflora. The most important observation regarding equol is that most adults do not produce equol, even when challenged with high doses of soy.338 This is a contrast to nonhuman primates and other animals; all that have been studied produce high levels of equol. Thus, there are two human populations: equol producers and nonequol producers. The key question is whether equol producers receive greater clinical effects from phytoestrogens than nonequol producers. As noted, thus far the clinical effects of isoflavones on bone have not been impressive. In a 2-year randomized trial of postmenopausal women, isoflavone-rich soy milk increased spinal bone mass in the 45% of the subjects who were equol producers, with essentially no effect in nonequol producers.338 Similar results were obtained in a Japanese trial testing the effect of isoflavone treatment in equol producers and nonproducers.339 Therefore, the population destined to receive a benefit from soy intake may be limited to equol producers. Studies need to be repeated measuring the responses in individuals who are identified as equol producers or nonequol producers. If the population destined to receive a benefit from soy intake is limited to equol producers, a convenient, inexpensive method must be developed to identify equol production. It may be possible to convert nonproducers to producers. A more straightforward approach is to administer equol itself. Daidzein yields two forms in equol producers, the *R*-equol inactive isomer and *S*-equol, the active isomer that binds to estrogen receptor-β. *S*-equol has been synthesized and its administration is effective for the treatment of menopausal symptoms.340 Another alternative is the *S*-equol supplement made by incubating equol-producing bacteria with soy isoflavones.341,342 It is absorbed readily with high bioavailability; low doses must be administered twice daily because of a relatively short half-life.

**Statins**

Statin treatment is, of course, a mainstay in programs designed to prevent cardiovascular disease. Prompted by animal experiments indicating that statins increase bone formation, case-control studies reported that statin treatment was associated with a reduced risk of fracture.343,344 However, analysis of a randomized trial of statin therapy and cardiovascular disease
